\title{Together, glider pilots find the way}

\author{Matteo Di Sante}
\affiliation{\pending{affiliation}}

\begin{abstract}
Soaring pilots and birds travel across country by chaining invisible, short-lived thermals, and
they often fly in groups. Whether company helps is hard to measure in the field, since groups
form where flying is good. We identify groups in \LtFlights{} cross-country paraglider flights
recorded in France between \LtFirstYear{} and \LtLastYear{} and compare group and solo flight in
three designs that remove first the route and then the pilot. Raw, pilots in a group move more
persistently over times longer than one glide and climb, and their transitions between climbs have
a heavier tail: the longest follow lines of rising air for tens of kilometres. On the
same route, once the pilots who fly in groups are compared with themselves alone, the gap in
persistence vanishes; within a single flight, persistence rises when a pilot joins a group and
falls back when they leave. Followers turn where the leader turned, and the gain grows with the
relief, from plains and hills to high mountains. A group helps a pilot by putting them on the right
route.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

Thermal soaring turns the atmosphere into an energy landscape~\cite{Shepard2013}: a bird or a
glider gains height by circling in rising air and spends it gliding to the next
thermal~\cite{Akos2008,Keskin2025}. Thermals are invisible, they form and decay within tens of
minutes, and where they rise is only partly predictable from the ground, so that learning to
find them is a problem of its own~\cite{Reddy2016,Reddy2018}. A cross-country flight is a search,
and its statistics carry the signature of the search: over hours the horizontal motion is
superdiffusive, close to ballistic, with a Hurst exponent near 0.9 for paragliders, hang gliders
and sailplanes alike~\cite{Vilpellet2026}.

Soaring birds use each other to find lift. Vultures informed by others about the next updraft
start their glides faster~\cite{Williams2018}, and vultures flying freely rely on the thermals
others use, more so in poor conditions~\cite{Sassi2024}. In a migrating flock of white storks a
few leaders find and explore the thermals, while followers leave them earlier and lower and flap
more~\cite{Flack2018}. Groups can follow gradients that single individuals cannot
sense~\cite{Berdahl2013}, and pooling the noisy headings of many individuals improves
navigation~\cite{Simons2004,Codling2007,Berdahl2018}. Whether flying together makes soaring flight
more efficient has not been measured, for birds or for pilots. The obstacle is selection: groups
form where and when flying is good, and those who join them are not a random sample of
individuals~\cite{Manski1993}. A raw comparison of group and solo flight mixes the effect of the
group with those of the route, the day and the pilot.

Here we measure it on human pilots. We find the pilots who fly together with a rule calibrated
against chance encounters, and compare group and solo flight three times: as they are; on the same
route, with the same pilots alone as a control; and within one flight, before, during and after
a group. The three comparisons agree: the group straightens a pilot's route by putting them on a
better one.

\textit{Data and groups.} The French free-flight federation (FFVL) keeps the tracks of the
cross-country flights its pilots declare. We use the \LtFlights{} paraglider flights recorded
between \LtFirstYear{} and \LtLastYear{} with a logger of at most \LtLoggerMax~s. Two pilots are
in contact when one passes where the other passed less than \LtDeltaMin~min earlier, within
\LtEpsH~m horizontally and \LtEpsZ~m vertically, with headings within \LtTheta$^\circ$ in glide,
and the contact lasts at least $\tau^* = \tauStar$~min. We chose $\tau^*$ as the duration at which
surrogate encounters, made by shifting flights in time, account for at most \LtNullShare\% of the
contacts (Supplemental Material [SM]).
A group is a set of pilots linked by contacts, with the same members over an interval. Then
\LtGroupFlights{} flights (\LtGroupShare\%) fly in a group at some point, for \LtGroupHours{} hours
in \LtEpisodes{} episodes, \LtPairShare\% of that time in pairs (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). We compare
three populations: the group time of these flights (G), the same flights while alone (A) and the
flights never in a group (S). Every flight is split into climbs, searches and transitions by the
labelling of Ref.~\cite{Vilpellet2026}. Persistence is read from the time-averaged mean squared
displacement of the horizontal position through its local exponent
\begin{equation}
\Hloc(\tau) = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{d \ln \mathrm{MSD}(\tau)}{d \ln \tau},
\end{equation}
which is $1/2$ for diffusion and 1 for straight flight~\cite{Metzler2014}. The comparisons use the
flights that end away from their takeoff, since closed circuits bend the MSD at long lags [SM]. All
bands are 90\% intervals of a bootstrap over pilots and over episodes and site-days, combined as
in Ref.~\cite{Cameron2011}; on synthetic data with no group effect they cover zero in
\CovNewMin--\CovNewMax\% of the cases [SM].

\textit{Raw comparison.} Below \RawGapCross~s pilots in a group are slightly less persistent than
solo pilots; beyond, they are more persistent, by \RawGapHK{} \RawGapHKBand{} at $\tau = 1000$~s
(Fig.~\ref{fig:persistence}a). The crossover is the duration of one leg: the median glide lasts
\RawGlideMedianG~s and the median climb \RawClimbMedianG~s, in G as in S. Over a manoeuvre the group
costs a little; over the succession of thermals that makes a route, it pays. A search in a group
also ends in a climb more often, \RawStatFoundG\% of the time against \RawStatFoundS\% alone.

The transitions between climbs last longer in a group (Fig.~\ref{fig:tail}). Above \LtTailXmin~s
the Hill index of their durations~\cite{Hill1975} is \LtTailMuG{} \LtTailMuGBand{} for G against
\LtTailMuS{} \LtTailMuSBand{} for S, and the two bands stay apart for every threshold between
\LtTailApartFrom{} and \LtTailApartTo~s; climbs and searches show no such difference [SM]. Since the
index changes with the threshold, we use it only to compare the weight of the tails. The longest
transitions are flights along lines of rising air: those above \LtLongMin~s cover about
\LtLongPathG~km in a group with a median vertical speed of \LtLongVzG~m/s, rising \LtLongRisingG\%
of the time. In a group they cost less height, \LtLongSinkG{} against \LtLongSinkS~m per km of net
displacement, a gap of \LtLongSinkGapG{} \LtLongSinkGapGBand{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:tail}c). The same
flights alone lie in between, \LtLongSinkGapA{} \LtLongSinkGapABand{} from solo flights, so about
half of the gap comes with the pilots and the days; the gain in vertical speed, \LtLongVzGapG{}
\LtLongVzGapGBand~m/s, appears only in the group (alone, \LtLongVzGapA{} \LtLongVzGapABand).

\textit{Same route.} Part of this gap is the route. We matched every piece of group flight to
pieces of solo flights along the same route (a share \MatCover{} of the points within \MatRho~m,
same direction), within \MatMonth{} months of the date and \MatHour{} hours of the time of day, on a
wing of the same class [SM].
On the same route the gap at 1000~s falls to \MatDeltaHKG{} \MatDeltaHKGBand. The pilots who fly in
groups are more persistent also when alone on those routes, by \MatDeltaHKA{} at 1000~s and
\MatDeltaHQA{} \MatDeltaHQABand{} at 1840~s. The effect of the group at fixed route and pilot is the
difference of the two: \MatDeltaHKD{} \MatDeltaHKDBand{} at 1000~s and \MatDeltaHQD{}
\MatDeltaHQDBand{} at 1840~s. On a given route a pilot in a group is as persistent as alone. Over
all flights the route accounts for \RouteExplained\% of the raw gap, for every matching radius and
cover we tried [SM]. On the same route the tails of the transitions of G and of its matched solo
pieces cannot be told apart, though only \LtTailRouteRunsG{} group transitions exceed
\LtTailXmin~s there [SM].

\textit{Same flight.} Within one flight the route changes, and the company with it. In the flights
that join a group and later leave it, we cut four windows of \PreWindow~s: before the group, at its
start, at its end and after it [SM]. The changes at entry, $D_\mathrm{in}$, and at exit,
$D_\mathrm{out}$, have a mean $g = (D_\mathrm{in} + D_\mathrm{out})/2$ free of any linear drift along
the flight. Persistence at 1000~s rises by $g = \PreGHK$ \PreGHKBand{} in the group and returns
when the pilot leaves: after minus before is \PrePPHK{} \PrePPHKBand{}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:persistence}c). The drift along the flight, $d_w = (D_\mathrm{in} -
D_\mathrm{out})/2$, is \PreDwHK{} \PreDwHKBand. The band
of the effect at fixed route, \MatDeltaHKDBand, stays below this jump: most of the jump is the line
the group flies.

\textit{How the line passes.} Groups fly as files along the route. The pilot ahead changes
\StSwitches{} times per hour, and the lead goes more often to the more experienced pilot
(\StExp\%) and to the higher wing class (\StWing\%). Followers turn where the leader turned: the
correlation of the headings of a pair peaks at a time lag equal to their delay along the route, at
every delay (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}a). Lift is tied to places: a place where a glider climbed keeps
producing climbs for about \StMkHalfGroup~min, and a glider climbing there now raises the rate of
new climbs by a factor of only \StMkNowGroup{} over one that climbed there five minutes earlier
[SM]. A route is a chain of such places, and a group carries it along. This is also what the group
adds on a given route. Net of selection, on the same route the time in climb grows by
\MatDeltaClimbD{} \MatDeltaClimbDBand{} percentage points. Within the flight, joining a group
raises the time in climb by \PreGClimb{} points and the share of searches that end in a climb by
\PreGFound{} points, and glides become faster by \PreGGlide~km/h and flatter by \PreGSink~m per km
(Fig.~\ref{fig:how}b).

\textit{Terrain.} If the group shows the route, it should matter most where the route is hard to
find. We classed every point of every flight by the local relief within about 10~km, following
Hammond's landform classes~\cite{Hammond1964} as computed from an elevation model~\cite{Dikau1991}
on the 25-m grid of the French mapping agency [SM]. The gain grows with the relief
(Fig.~\ref{fig:how}c). Raw, it is \LtTerRawLow{} \LtTerRawLowBand{} in plains and hills,
\LtTerRawLowMtn{} \LtTerRawLowMtnBand{} in low mountains and \LtTerRawHigh{} \LtTerRawHighBand{} in
high mountains. Within the same flight it is \LtTerFlightMtn{} \LtTerFlightMtnBand{} in mountains
and \LtTerFlightLow{} \LtTerFlightLowBand{} in plains and hills, where only
\LtTerFlightLowEvents{} events of \LtTerFlightLowPilots{} pilots are available. Alone, a pilot in
plains and hills already flies nearly straight, with $\Hloc(1000~\mathrm{s}) = \LtTerHlocSLow$
against \LtTerHlocSHigh{} in high mountains, so there is less to gain there; in high mountains,
where the relief bends the route, the group straightens it most. In plains and hills routes
rarely repeat from one day to another: only \LtTerRouteLowPieces{} pieces of group flight find a
solo flight on the same route.

\textit{Discussion.} Two features of the effect point to a group that shows the way. It appears
only beyond one leg, where the choice of the next thermal matters, and it lasts as long as the
group. The tail of the transitions shows what a good route contains: lines of rising air along
which a pilot covers tens of kilometres without circling. Groups fly more of them; on the same route
our data cannot tell whether they fly them farther. On a given route the group changes the phases: more time in climb, faster and flatter
glides. Faster glides are what speed-to-fly theory prescribes when the next climb is surer or
stronger~\cite{MacCready1954,Cochrane1999}, and what vultures do when others reveal the next
thermal~\cite{Williams2018}. In storks, followers gain from leaders who find the
thermals~\cite{Flack2018}; among pilots, the lead goes to the more experienced. Our data do not
fully separate a group that leads pilots to the good line from a group that gathers on it: groups
form mostly as chance meetings on shared lines, pairs attracting only \StEnSoloPair{} times more
than independent meetings [SM]. Either way, for a pilot the group is where the route is. Comparing
one flight with itself, before, during and after a group, could measure the value of company for
soaring birds tagged in flocks.
