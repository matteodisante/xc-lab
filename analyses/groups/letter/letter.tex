\title{The value of flying together}

\author{Matteo Di Sante}
\affiliation{\pending{affiliation}}

\begin{abstract}
Soaring pilots and birds travel across country by chaining invisible, short-lived thermals, and
they often fly in groups. Whether company helps is hard to measure in the field, since groups form
where and when flying is good. We identify groups in \LtFlights{} cross-country paraglider flights
recorded in France between \LtFirstYear{} and \LtLastYear{}, with a rule calibrated against chance
encounters, and compare group and solo flight in three designs. In a group the horizontal motion is
more persistent, at time lags from tens of seconds to three quarters of an hour, and cross-country
speed is higher. The gap remains against solo pilots who flew the same route in the same season and
at the same hour, but the flights that join groups show a gap of similar size while they fly alone,
so this comparison does not isolate the group. Within a single flight, persistence and speed rise when a pilot joins a group and fall back
when they leave, a jump that flights without a group do not show at the same times of flight.
Followers retrace the line of the pilot ahead, and lift stays tied to places for tens of minutes:
what company provides may be the route.
\end{abstract}

\section*{INTRODUCTION}

Thermal soaring turns the atmosphere into an energy landscape~\cite{Shepard2013}: a bird or a
glider gains height by circling in rising air and spends it gliding to the next
thermal~\cite{Akos2008,Keskin2025}. Thermals are invisible, they form and decay within tens of
minutes, and where they rise is only partly predictable from the ground, so that learning to
find them is a problem of its own~\cite{Reddy2016,Reddy2018}. A cross-country flight is a search,
and its statistics carry the signature of the search: over hours the horizontal motion is
superdiffusive, close to ballistic, with a Hurst exponent close to 0.9 for paragliders, hang
gliders and sailplanes alike~\cite{Vilpellet2026}.

Soaring birds use each other to find lift. Vultures informed by others about the next updraft
glide faster between thermals~\cite{Williams2018}, and griffon vultures flown freely rely on the
thermals others use, more so in poor conditions~\cite{Sassi2024}. In a migrating flock of white
storks a few leaders find and explore the thermals, while followers leave them earlier and lower
and flap more~\cite{Flack2018}. Groups can follow gradients that single individuals cannot
sense~\cite{Berdahl2013}, and pooling the noisy headings of many individuals improves
navigation~\cite{Simons2004,Codling2007,Berdahl2018}. Whether flying together makes soaring flight
more efficient has not been measured, for birds or for pilots. The obstacle is selection: groups
form where and when flying is good, and those who join them are not a random sample of
individuals~\cite{Manski1993}. A raw comparison of group and solo flight mixes the effect of the
group with those of the route, the day and the pilot.

Here we measure it on human pilots. We find the pilots who fly together with a rule calibrated
against chance encounters, and compare group and solo flight three times: as they are; on the same
route, against solo pilots who flew it, with the flights that join groups taken while alone as a
control for selection; and within one flight, before, during and after a group, against mock
events placed at the same times of flight in flights without a group. The three designs agree: in
company pilots fly straighter and faster, and within a flight the gain comes and goes with the
group.

\section*{DATA AND GROUPS}

The French free-flight federation (FFVL) keeps the tracks of the cross-country flights its pilots
declare. We use the \LtFlights{} paraglider flights recorded between \LtFirstYear{} and
\LtLastYear{} with a logger of at most \LtLoggerMax~s. Two pilots are in contact when one passes
where the other passed shortly before, on a similar heading, and the contact lasts at least
$\tau^* = \tauStar$~min. We chose $\tau^*$ as the duration at which surrogate encounters, made by
shifting flights in time, account for at most \LtNullShare\% of the contacts
(Appendix~\ref{app:groups}, \SM{4}). A group is a set of pilots linked by contacts, directly or
through other pilots, with the same members over an interval (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). Then
\LtGroupShare\% of the flights fly in a group at some point, \LtPairShare\% of that time in pairs.
We compare three populations: the group time of these flights (G), the same flights while alone (A)
and the flights never in a group (S).

Of the \LtOpenTotal{} flights, the comparisons use the \LtOpenKept{} that the pilot declared open
and whose opening is above \LtOpenThreshold. The opening is the distance between takeoff and
landing over the length of the path. Closed circuits bend the mean squared displacement at long
lags, and the threshold removes those declared open that return near their takeoff (\SM{1}).

\section*{MEASURING PERSISTENCE}

Persistence is read from the time-averaged mean squared displacement (MSD) of the horizontal
position. We write
\begin{equation}
\alpha(\tau) = \frac{d \ln \mathrm{MSD}(\tau)}{d \ln \tau}
\end{equation}
for the local slope of the MSD. It is 1 for diffusion and 2 for straight
flight~\cite{Metzler2014}. We estimate it on a window of $\pm\LtDecade$ decades around $\tau$
(\SM{3}). The exponent $H$ is the one fitted from \LtFitFrom~s to the end lag, $\mathrm{MSD}\sim
\tau^{2H}$, so that $\alpha = 2H$ for a power law. We report both. Every flight logged at 1~Hz is
also split into climbs, searches and transitions by the labelling of Ref.~\cite{Vilpellet2026};
the statistics of phases rest on these flights (\SM{8}).

A lag $\tau$ needs pieces of at least $\tau/\LtSupport$, so that the origins of a piece cover the
lag in the same share of it. A dataset to the lag $T$ keeps the pieces of at least
$T/\LtSupport$ and measures every lag up to $T$ on them, so that the population does not change
along a curve. The main dataset has $T = \LtTauTwo$~s, with pieces of at least \RawShortestTwo~s.
The check has $T = \LtTauOne$~s, with pieces of at least \RawShortestOne~s. We stop at
\LtTauTwo~s for two reasons. Beyond it the same-route design has too few matched pieces of G: at
$T = \LtLongerTau$~s no caliper reaches \LtMinPieces{} pieces per level of the wing. And longer
pieces select flights with a larger exponent: on the dataset to \LtTauLong~s, $\aK$ of solo flights
is \LtRawAlphaKSLong{}, against \LtRawAlphaKSTwo{} on the main dataset. The level shifts and the
shape of $\alpha(\tau)$ at the shared lags is kept (\SM{2}).

All bands are \LtBandLevel\% intervals of a bootstrap over pilots and over blocks of episodes and
site-days, combined as in Ref.~\cite{Cameron2011} (Appendix~\ref{app:bands}). On synthetic data
with no group effect they hold zero in \CovNewMin--\CovNewMax\% of the cases (\SM{5}).

\section*{RAW COMPARISON}

On the main dataset group time is more persistent than solo flight at every lag from
\LtRawClearTwo~s to the end (Fig.~\ref{fig:msd}, Fig.~\ref{fig:designs}a). The gap is small at the
shortest lags and largest near \LtLagOne~s, on both datasets: longer pieces shift its level and keep
its shape. At \LtLagOne~s $\alpha$
differs by \LtRawAlphaGapKGSTwo{} \LtRawAlphaGapKGSTwoBand{} and the fitted $H$ by
\LtRawFitGapGSTwo{} (Table~\ref{tab:designs}). On the shorter pieces of the dataset to
\LtTauOne~s the gap opens only beyond \LtRawClearOne~s. The flights that join groups, while alone,
have the $\alpha$ of solo flights (A $-$ S is \LtRawAlphaGapKASTwo{} at \LtLagOne~s) but are
faster by \LtRawXcGapAS{}~km/h over \LtXcMin~min, against \LtRawXcGapGS{}~km/h for group time. This
is a first sign of selection.

\section*{SAME ROUTE}

We matched every piece of G to pieces of solo flights along the same route, within \MatMonth{}
months, within \MatHour~h of the hour and on a wing of the same class (\SM{6}). The solo pieces come
from other pilots, who share only the route with the group. The gap remains. On the main dataset
G $-$ S$|$G is \LtRouteAlphaKGSTwo{} at \LtLagOne~s and \LtRouteAlphaQGSTwo{} at \LtLagTwo~s
(Fig.~\ref{fig:designs}b, Table~\ref{tab:designs}), no smaller than the raw gap of
\LtRawAlphaGapKGSTwo{}. On the check dataset it is \LtRouteAlphaKGSOne{}, about half the raw gap
of \LtRawAlphaGapKGSOne{}. The flights that join groups, taken while alone and matched in the same
way, keep about half of the gap at \LtLagOne~s, where A $-$ S$|$A is \LtRouteAlphaKASTwo{}
\LtRouteAlphaKASTwoBand, and all of it at \LtLagTwo~s (\LtRouteAlphaQASTwo{}
\LtRouteAlphaQASTwoBand). This part is selection. The effect of the group on the same route, net of
selection, is the difference of the two. For $\alpha$ and for the fitted $H$ it is not resolved on
the main dataset: at \LtLagOne~s it is \LtRouteAlphaKDiffTwo{} \LtRouteAlphaKDiffTwoBand, a band
that includes zero and values as large as the raw gap, so the design does not decide. On the check
dataset the difference of $\alpha$ is not resolved either (\LtRouteAlphaKDiffOne{}
\LtRouteAlphaKDiffOneBand), and that of the fitted $H$ is \LtRouteFitDiffOne{}
\LtRouteFitDiffOneBand, small and of the opposite sign (\SM{6}). For speed it is resolved: net of
selection the group gains \LtRouteXcDiff{} \LtRouteXcDiffBand~km/h over \LtXcMin~min, on
\LtRouteXcPiecesG{} pieces of G and \LtRouteXcPiecesA{} of A (\SM{8}). The sign of G $-$ S$|$G holds
over the \LtRobLines{} lines of the match (\SM{12}). The main dataset keeps \LtRoutePiecesGTwo{}
pieces of G and \LtRoutePiecesATwo{} of A, and the check dataset \LtRoutePiecesGOne{} and
\LtRoutePiecesAOne{}.

\section*{SAME FLIGHT}

Within one flight the pilot, the glider and the day are fixed. In the flights that join a group and
later leave it, we cut four windows: before the group, at its start, at its end and after it. The
changes at entry, $D_\mathrm{in}$, and at exit, $D_\mathrm{out}$, have a mean $g =
(D_\mathrm{in} + D_\mathrm{out})/2$ free of any linear drift along the flight, and a half
difference $d_w = (D_\mathrm{in} - D_\mathrm{out})/2$ that measures this drift (\SM{7}). A
profile with curvature along the flight is not removed by $g$. Mock events test for it. A mock
event is the same four windows placed at the same times of flight in a flight never in a group,
within \MatMonth{} months, \MatHour~h of the hour and a wing of the same class as the real event.

Net of the mock events, $\alpha$ rises by \LtFltMaxNetGAlphaK{} at \LtLagOne~s and by
\LtFltMaxNetGAlphaQ{} at \LtLagTwo~s when the group begins and falls back when it ends
(Fig.~\ref{fig:designs}c). The fitted $H$ rises by \LtFltMaxNetGFit{} and the speed by
\LtFltMaxNetGXc{}~km/h, on \LtFltMaxEvents{} events with windows of \LtWinMax~s. With windows of
\LtWinMid~s, on \LtFltMidEvents{} events, the change at \LtLagOne~s is the same
(\LtFltMidNetGAlphaK{}). The drift $d_w$ is compatible with zero on both. The mock events are
small but not always zero: they give \LtFltMaxMockGXc{}~km/h of speed. Mock events drawn at the
takeoff site of the real event give a net change of \LtFltMaxNetSiteGAlphaK{} at \LtLagOne~s. On
the windows of \LtWinMax~s their net change at \LtLagTwo~s (\LtFltMaxNetSiteGAlphaQ{}) and in speed
(\LtFltMaxNetSiteGXc{}~km/h) has a band that includes zero (\SM{7}). In
the phases of flight, with windows of \LtWinMid~s and net of the mock events, the time in climb
rises by \PreNetGClimb{} percentage points, the share of searches that end in a climb by
\PreNetGFound{} points, glides become faster by \PreNetGGlide{}~km/h and the height lost per km
changes by \PreNetGSink{}~m/km, and the climb rate is not resolved (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}b, \SM{8}).

\section*{HOW A GROUP HOLDS A ROUTE}

Lift is tied to places. We call the start of a climb marked when a glider outside the group climbs
within \StMkR~m of it in the \StMkDT~s before. The share of marked starts is \StMkNowGroup{} times
its value when the same gliders are shifted by \StMkNear~min before or after: a glider climbing
right then adds little to what the place gives. The share falls to half of its value at no shift
only after \StMkHalfGroup~min, so a place keeps being used for climbs for at least that long
(\SM{11}). A route is a chain of such places. Groups fly it as files. The pilot ahead changes
\StSwitches{} times per hour of contact, less often than the \StSwitchesChance{} of chance
contacts of the same duration. The lead goes slightly more often to the pilot with the higher wing
class (\StWing\% of the pairs) or with more flights in the archive (\StExp\%), two markers of
experience. Followers turn where the leader turned: the correlation of the headings of a pair
peaks at a time lag close to their delay along the route in the four classes of delay, from
\StHdLOne{} to \StHdLFour~s, with no second peak near zero lag from \StHdLTwo~s of delay
(Fig.~\ref{fig:how}a). A follower retraces the line and does not answer the leader's turns, and
surrogate pairs, who never saw each other, correlate as much from \StHdLChanceThree~s of delay. A
solo glider that meets an existing pair joins it \StEnSoloPair{} times more often than expected
from the number of gliders, and one that meets a group of three or more as often as expected
(\StEnSoloThree{}).

On the same route, net of selection, the time in climb rises by \MatDeltaClimbD{} percentage points
\MatDeltaClimbDBand, and the glide speed and the height lost per km are not resolved
(\SM{8}). The gain is largest in the high mountains. We classed every point
by the relief within a square window of \TnWindowKm~km and gave a piece the class of at least
\TnMajority\% of its points (\SM{10}). On the check dataset, raw, $\Delta\aK$ is \LtTerRawLow{} in plains and
hills, \LtTerRawLowMtn{} in low mountains and \LtTerRawHigh{} in high mountains
(Fig.~\ref{fig:how}c). Within the flight, with windows of \LtWinMid~s, it is \LtTerFlightMtn{} in the mountains, on \LtTerFlightMtnEvents{} events, and
not resolved in plains and hills, on \LtTerFlightLowEvents{} events. Alone, a pilot in plains and
hills already flies nearly straight, with $\alpha(\LtLagOne\,\mathrm{s}) = \LtTerAlphaSLow$ for S
against \LtTerAlphaSHigh{} in high mountains.

The transitions between climbs last longer in group time (Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}). We compare the
tails of their durations through the Hill estimator~\cite{Hill1975},
\begin{equation}
\hat\mu = n\Bigl/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\frac{x_i}{x_\mathrm{min}},
\end{equation}
of the survival $P(X>x)\sim x^{-\mu}$ over the $n$ durations $x_i$ above $x_\mathrm{min}$. We read
every population at one threshold, $x_\mathrm{min} = \TlXmin$~s, chosen on S by the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance~\cite{Clauset2009}; the bands are those of Appendix~\ref{app:bands} at
fixed threshold, and the index changes with the threshold (\SM{9}). Raw, the tail of group time is
heavier, $\hat\mu = \TlRawMuG$ against \TlRawMuS{} for solo flights (Table~\ref{tab:tail}). The
other two designs do not confirm it. On the same route the effect of the group on $\hat\mu$ cannot
be told from zero, with a wide band. Within the flight, the transitions flown entirely in a group
have the index of those flown before and after it, and the heavy tail of group time comes from the
transitions that cross the start or the end of a group. The long transitions of a group cost less
height per km, and so do those of solo pilots on the same route: a long, cheap glide belongs to the
line.

\section*{DISCUSSION}

In company, paraglider pilots fly straighter and faster. A raw comparison could not establish it,
since groups form on good days and good routes and gather pilots who fly well. Two results carry
the claim. Against solo pilots on the same route the gap remains. And within one flight
persistence and speed rise at the entry into a group and return at the exit, while flights
without a group show no such jump at the same times of flight. How much of the gap belongs to the
group is less certain: on the same route the flights that join groups keep half of it or more when
they fly alone, and the remainder is resolved for speed and not for persistence
(Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}).

What does company provide? Our data favour the route over the instant. Lift stays tied to places
for tens of minutes, and a glider climbing now adds little to what the place gives. Followers
retrace the line of the pilot ahead and do not answer its turns. Long glides that cost little
height are found by solo pilots on the same route as well. Faster glides are what speed-to-fly
theory prescribes when the next climb is surer or stronger~\cite{MacCready1954,Cochrane1999}, and
what vultures do when others reveal the next thermal~\cite{Williams2018}. We cannot tell a group
that leads its members to a good line from a group that gathers on it. Our groups are defined by
flight alone: pilots who arranged to fly together, who talk by radio or who follow the known
routes of a site are among them, and the archive does not tell them apart.

\section*{CONCLUDING REMARKS}

The same-flight design needs only tracks and a rule for who flies with whom. Each flight is its
own control, and mock events remove what the course of a flight produces by itself. It applies as
it is to soaring birds tagged in flocks, where the value of company has been read from leaders
and followers~\cite{Flack2018} or from single thermals~\cite{Williams2018,Sassi2024}. For pilots
it puts a number on a question asked at every takeoff: within a flight, company is worth about
\LtFltMaxNetGXc{}~km/h of cross-country speed.
