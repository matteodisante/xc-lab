\title{Good company is a good route}

\author{Authors}
\affiliation{\pending{affiliation}}

\begin{abstract}
Soaring pilots and birds travel across country by chaining invisible, short-lived thermals, and
they often fly in groups. Whether company helps is hard to measure, since groups form where and
when flying is good and gather those who fly well. We identify groups in \LtFlights{}
cross-country paraglider flights recorded in France between \LtFirstYear{} and \LtLastYear{}, with
a rule calibrated against chance encounters, and compare group and solo flight three times, each
time removing more of this selection. Speed survives all three. On the same route, in the same
season and at the same hour, the pilots who join groups are no faster than solo pilots when they
fly alone, and in a group they gain \LtRouteXcDiff~km/h, \LtRouteXcDiffPct\% of the speed of their
solo controls. Within a single flight, where the pilot, the wing and the day are fixed, speed
rises by \LtFltMaxNetGXc~km/h when a pilot joins a group and falls back when the pilot leaves, a
jump that flights without a group do not show at the same times of flight. The motion also
becomes more persistent within the flight, while on the same route the pilots who join groups are
more persistent even alone. Lift stays tied to places for tens of minutes and followers retrace
the line of the pilot ahead: what company provides seems to be the route.
\end{abstract}

\section*{INTRODUCTION}

Thermal soaring turns the atmosphere into an energy landscape~\cite{Shepard2013}: a bird or a
glider gains height by circling in rising air and spends it gliding to the next
thermal~\cite{Akos2008,Keskin2025}. Thermals are invisible, they form and decay within tens of
minutes, and where they rise is only partly predictable from the ground, so that learning to
find them is a problem of its own~\cite{Reddy2016,Reddy2018}. A cross-country flight is a search,
and its statistics carry the signature of the search: over hours the horizontal motion is
superdiffusive, close to ballistic, with a Hurst exponent close to 0.9 for paragliders, hang
gliders and sailplanes alike~\cite{Vilpellet2026}.

Soaring birds use each other to find lift. Vultures informed by others about the next updraft
glide faster between thermals~\cite{Williams2018}, and griffon vultures flown freely rely on the
thermals others use, more so in poor conditions~\cite{Sassi2024}. In a migrating flock of white
storks a few leaders find and explore the thermals, while followers leave them earlier and lower
and flap more~\cite{Flack2018}. Groups can follow gradients that single individuals cannot
sense~\cite{Berdahl2013}, and pooling the noisy headings of many individuals improves
navigation~\cite{Simons2004,Codling2007,Berdahl2018}. Whether flying together makes soaring flight
more efficient has not been measured, for birds or for pilots. The obstacle is selection: groups
form where and when flying is good, and those who join them are not a random sample of
individuals~\cite{Manski1993}. A raw comparison of group and solo flight mixes the effect of the
group with those of the route, the day and the pilot.

Here we measure it on human pilots. We find the pilots who fly together with a rule calibrated
against chance encounters, and compare group and solo flight three times: as they are; on the same
route, against solo pilots who flew it, with the flights that join groups taken while alone as a
control for selection; and within one flight, before, during and after a group, against mock
events placed at the same times of flight in flights without a group. Each design removes more of
the selection than the one before, and the claim narrows as it goes. Speed survives all three: in
company pilots fly faster, by about as much on the same route as within one flight, and within a
flight the gain comes and goes with the group. Persistence survives the raw comparison and the
comparison within the flight, while on the same route the pilots who join groups carry much of it
when they fly alone. Where the gain comes from points to the route the group holds. The last
design needs only tracks and a rule for who flies with whom, and applies as it is to soaring birds
tagged in flocks.

\section*{DATA AND GROUPS}

The French free-flight federation (FFVL) keeps the tracks of the cross-country flights its pilots
declare. We use the \LtFlights{} paraglider flights recorded between \LtFirstYear{} and
\LtLastYear{} with a logger of at most \LtLoggerMax~s. Two pilots are in contact when one passes
where the other passed shortly before, on a similar heading, and the contact lasts at least
$\tau^* = \tauStar$~min. We chose $\tau^*$ as the duration at which surrogate encounters, made by
shifting flights in time, account for at most \LtNullShare\% of the contacts
(Appendix~\ref{app:groups}, \SM{4}). A group is a set of pilots linked by contacts, directly or
through other pilots, with the same members over an interval (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). Then
\LtGroupShare\% of the flights fly in a group at some point, \LtPairShare\% of that time in pairs.
We compare three populations: the group time of these flights (G), the same flights while alone (A)
and the flights never in a group (S).

Of the \LtOpenTotal{} flights, the comparisons use the \LtOpenKept{} that the pilot declared open
and whose opening is above \LtOpenThreshold. The opening is the distance between takeoff and
landing over the length of the path. Closed circuits bend the mean squared displacement at long
lags, and the threshold removes those declared open that return near their takeoff (\SM{1}).

\section*{MEASURING PERSISTENCE}

Persistence is read from the time-averaged mean squared displacement (MSD) of the horizontal
position. We write
\begin{equation}
\alpha(\tau) = \frac{d \ln \mathrm{MSD}(\tau)}{d \ln \tau}
\end{equation}
for the local slope of the MSD. It is 1 for diffusion and 2 for straight
flight~\cite{Metzler2014}. We estimate it on a window of $\pm\LtDecade$ decades around $\tau$
(\SM{3}). The exponent $H$ is the one fitted from \LtFitFrom~s to the end lag, $\mathrm{MSD}\sim
\tau^{2H}$, so that $\alpha = 2H$ for a power law. We report both. Every flight logged at 1~Hz is
also split into climbs, searches and transitions by the labelling of Ref.~\cite{Vilpellet2026};
the statistics of phases rest on these flights (\SM{8}).

Every lag of a curve is measured on the same pieces, so that the population does not change along
it. The main dataset keeps the pieces of at least \RawShortestTwo~s and reads lags up to
\LtTauTwo~s; the check dataset keeps those of at least \RawShortestOne~s and reads lags up to
\LtTauOne~s. Longer lags leave too few matched pieces on the same route and select straighter
flights (\SM{2}).

All bands are \LtBandLevel\% intervals of a bootstrap over pilots and over blocks of episodes and
site-days, combined as in Ref.~\cite{Cameron2011} (Appendix~\ref{app:bands}). On synthetic data
with no group effect they hold zero in \CovNewMin--\CovNewMax\% of the cases (\SM{5}).

\section*{RAW COMPARISON}

What does a raw comparison show? On the main dataset group time is more persistent than solo
flight at every lag from \LtRawClearTwo~s to the end (Fig.~\ref{fig:msd},
Fig.~\ref{fig:designs}a). The gap is small at the shortest lags and largest near \LtLagOne~s, where
$\alpha$ differs by \LtRawAlphaGapKGSTwo{} \LtRawAlphaGapKGSTwoBand{}; the fitted $H$ differs by
\LtRawFitGapGSTwo{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}). On the shorter pieces of the check dataset the gap
opens only beyond \LtRawClearOne~s. Up to a few minutes the order changes: the flights that join
groups, while alone, are the most persistent of the three (Fig.~\ref{fig:msd}b). Group time is also faster, by \LtRawXcGapGS{}~km/h over
\LtXcMin~min. The flights that join groups, while alone, have the $\alpha$ of solo flights at
\LtLagOne~s (A $-$ S is \LtRawAlphaGapKASTwo{}), a fitted $H$ larger by \LtRawFitGapASTwo{}
\LtRawFitGapASTwoBand, and are faster by \LtRawXcGapAS{}~km/h. The pilots who join groups fly
better even alone, and the raw gap mixes them with the group.

\section*{SAME ROUTE}

Does the gap belong to the route? We matched every piece of G to pieces of solo flights along the
same route, within \MatMonth{} months, within \MatHour~h of the hour and on a wing of the same
class (\SM{6}). The solo pieces come from other pilots, who share only the route with the group.
Against them the gap of persistence remains: G $-$ S$|$G is \LtRouteAlphaKGSTwo{} at \LtLagOne~s
(Fig.~\ref{fig:designs}b, Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}), no smaller than the raw gap. The same match
applied to the flights that join groups, while alone, shows where it comes from: A $-$ S$|$A is
\LtRouteAlphaKASTwo{} \LtRouteAlphaKASTwoBand{} at \LtLagOne~s, half of the gap, and
\LtRouteAlphaQASTwo{} \LtRouteAlphaQASTwoBand{} at \LtLagTwo~s, all of it. These pilots fly
straighter than others on the same line even without company. Net of this selection, the effect
of the group on persistence, (G $-$ S$|$G) $-$ (A $-$ S$|$A), is \LtRouteAlphaKDiffTwo{}
\LtRouteAlphaKDiffTwoBand{} at \LtLagOne~s, and the design does not decide.

Speed tells a different story. Alone, the pilots who join groups are as fast as their controls (A
$-$ S$|$A is \LtRouteXcAS{} \LtRouteXcASBand~km/h). In a group they are faster, and net of
selection the group adds \LtRouteXcDiff{} \LtRouteXcDiffBand~km/h over \LtXcMin~min,
\LtRouteXcDiffPct\% of the speed of the solo controls, on \LtRouteXcPiecesG{} pieces of G and
\LtRouteXcPiecesA{} of A (\SM{8}). Their time in climb rises by
\MatDeltaClimbD{} percentage points \MatDeltaClimbDBand, while the glide speed and the height lost
per km are not resolved. The sign of G $-$ S$|$G holds over the \LtRobLines{} lines of the match
(\SM{12}). On the same route, company makes pilots faster; whether it makes them straighter, this
design cannot say.

\section*{SAME FLIGHT}

Does the gain follow the group within one flight? There the pilot, the glider and the day are
fixed. In the flights that join a group and later leave it, we cut four windows: before the
group, at its start, at its end and after it. The changes at entry, $D_\mathrm{in}$, and at exit,
$D_\mathrm{out}$, have a mean $g = (D_\mathrm{in} + D_\mathrm{out})/2$ free of any linear drift
along the flight, and a half difference $d_w = (D_\mathrm{in} - D_\mathrm{out})/2$ that measures
this drift (\SM{7}). A profile with curvature along the flight is not removed by $g$. Mock events
test for it: the same four windows placed at the same times of flight in a flight never in a
group, within \MatMonth{} months, \MatHour~h of the hour and a wing of the same class as the real
event.

Net of the mock events, speed rises by \LtFltMaxNetGXc{} \LtFltMaxNetGXcBand~km/h when the group
begins and falls back when it ends, on \LtFltMaxEvents{} events with windows of \LtWinMax~s.
Persistence follows: $\alpha$ rises by \LtFltMaxNetGAlphaK{} at \LtLagOne~s and by
\LtFltMaxNetGAlphaQ{} at \LtLagTwo~s (Fig.~\ref{fig:designs}c, Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}), and the
fitted $H$ by \LtFltMaxNetGFit. Windows of \LtWinMid~s, on \LtFltMidEvents{} events, give the same
change at \LtLagOne~s (\LtFltMidNetGAlphaK), and the drift $d_w$ is compatible with zero on both.
Mock events drawn at the takeoff site of the real event, which hold the place as well, leave
\LtFltMaxNetSiteGAlphaK{} at \LtLagOne~s; their change of speed, \LtFltMaxNetSiteGXc~km/h, has a
band that includes zero (\SM{7}). The phases show where the time goes (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}b,
\SM{8}). With windows of \LtWinMid~s and net of the mock events, the time in climb rises by
\PreNetGClimb{} percentage points, the share of searches that end in a climb by \PreNetGFound{}
points, glides become faster by \PreNetGGlide{}~km/h and the height lost per km changes by
\PreNetGSink{}~m/km, while the climb rate is not resolved. Within a flight, the gain comes and goes
with the group.

\section*{HOW A GROUP HOLDS A ROUTE}

What does a group give its members? Lift is tied to places. We call the start of a climb marked
when a glider outside the group climbs within \StMkR~m of it in the \StMkDT~s before. The share of
marked starts is \StMkNowGroup{} times its value when the same gliders are shifted by
\StMkNear~min before or after: a glider climbing right then adds little to what the place gives.
The share falls to half of its value at no shift only after \StMkHalfGroup~min (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}c), so a place keeps
being used for climbs for at least that long (\SM{11}). A route is a chain of such places. Groups
fly it as files. The pilot ahead changes \StSwitches{} times per hour of contact, less often than
the \StSwitchesChance{} of chance contacts of the same duration. The lead goes slightly more often
to the pilot with the higher wing class (\StWing\% of the pairs) or with more flights in the
archive (\StExp\%), two markers of experience. Followers turn where the leader turned: the
correlation of the headings of a pair peaks at a time lag close to their delay along the route in
the four classes of delay, from \StHdLOne{} to \StHdLFour~s, with no second peak near zero lag from
\StHdLTwo~s of delay (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}a). A follower retraces the line and does not answer the
leader's turns, and surrogate pairs, who never saw each other, correlate as much from
\StHdLChanceThree~s of delay. Pairs also attract. A solo glider that passes close to a pair joins
it \StEnSoloPair{} \StEnSoloPairBand{} times as often as if each glider of the pair were a separate
encounter between two solo gliders at the same distance. A group of three or more gets the joins
its size predicts (\StEnSoloThree{} \StEnSoloThreeBand). What a group holds is a line: places that
keep giving lift, and followers who retrace it.

\section*{WHERE COMPANY PAYS}

Is the gain the same everywhere? We classed every point by the relief within a square window of
\TnWindowKm~km and gave a piece the class of at least \TnMajority\% of its points (\SM{10}). On the
check dataset, raw, $\Delta\aK$ is \LtTerRawLow{} in plains and hills, \LtTerRawLowMtn{} in low
mountains and \LtTerRawHigh{} in high mountains. Within the flight, with
windows of \LtWinMid~s, it is \LtTerFlightMtn{} \LtTerFlightMtnBand{} in the mountains, on
\LtTerFlightMtnEvents{} events, and not resolved in plains and hills, on \LtTerFlightLowEvents{}
events. Alone, a pilot in plains and hills already flies with $\aK = \LtTerAlphaSLow$, against
\LtTerAlphaSHigh{} in high mountains. The gain in persistence is concentrated where solo pilots fly
least straight.

\section*{DISCUSSION}

In company, paraglider pilots fly faster, and within a flight they fly straighter. A raw
comparison could not establish it, since groups form on good days and good routes and gather
pilots who fly well. The two other designs remove this selection in different ways, and each
leaves something in. The same-route design holds the line and the hour but compares different
pilots. The same-flight design holds the pilot, the wing and the day, but a pilot joins a group
where groups are. On speed they agree: net of selection the group adds \LtRouteXcDiff{}
\LtRouteXcDiffBand~km/h on the same route, and \LtFltMaxNetGXc{} \LtFltMaxNetGXcBand~km/h within
the flight (Fig.~\ref{fig:forest}). On persistence they part. Within the flight $\aK$ rises by
\LtFltMaxNetGAlphaK{}, and by \LtFltMaxNetSiteGAlphaK{} against mock events drawn at the same
takeoff. On the same route the pilots who join groups keep half of the gap or more when they fly
alone, and what remains for the group is not resolved. Speed is the robust result; the gain in
persistence is seen only where the pilot is held fixed.

What does company provide? Our data favour the route over the instant. Lift stays tied to places
for tens of minutes, and a glider climbing right then adds little to what the place gives.
Followers retrace the line of the pilot ahead and do not answer its turns. Within the flight, in
the group, pilots spend more time climbing, more of their searches end in a climb, and their
glides are faster and cost less height per km. Faster glides are what speed-to-fly theory
prescribes when the next climb is surer or stronger~\cite{MacCready1954,Cochrane1999}, and what
vultures do when others reveal the next thermal~\cite{Williams2018}. A pilot who knows where the
line goes can afford to glide fast towards it. We cannot tell a group
that leads its members to a good line from a group that gathers on it. Our groups are defined by
flight alone: pilots who arranged to fly together, who talk by radio or who follow the known
routes of a site are among them, and the archive does not tell them apart.

\section*{CONCLUDING REMARKS}

The same-flight design needs only tracks and a rule for who flies with whom. Each flight is its
own control, and mock events remove what the course of a flight produces by itself. It applies as
it is to soaring birds tagged in flocks, where the value of company has been read from leaders
and followers~\cite{Flack2018} or from single thermals~\cite{Williams2018,Sassi2024}. For pilots
it puts a number on a question asked at every takeoff: within a flight, company is worth
\LtFltMaxNetGXc{} \LtFltMaxNetGXcBand~km/h of cross-country speed, and the same-route design,
which compares different pilots on the same line, finds as much: about \LtRouteXcDiffPct\% of the
speed of a solo pilot on the same line.
