\title{Good company is a good route}

\author{Matteo Di Sante}
\affiliation{\pending{affiliation}}

\begin{abstract}
Soaring pilots and birds travel across country by chaining invisible, short-lived thermals, and
they often fly in groups. Whether company helps is hard to measure in the field, since groups
form where flying is good. We identify groups in \LtFlights{} cross-country paraglider flights
recorded in France between \LtFirstYear{} and \LtLastYear{} and compare group and solo flight in
three designs that remove first the route and then the pilot. Raw, pilots in a group move more
persistently over times longer than one glide and climb. On the same route, once the pilots who fly
in groups are compared with themselves alone, the gap in persistence is no longer detectable, yet
the group still helps: pilots spend more time climbing and progress faster across country. Within a
single flight, persistence and speed rise when a pilot joins a group and fall back when they leave,
a jump absent at the same times of flight in flights without a group. Lift stays tied to places for
tens of minutes, groups meet on the lines that join these places, and followers retrace the line of
the pilot ahead. The gain grows with the relief, from plains and hills to high mountains. A group
gives a pilot the route, and on that route it makes them faster.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

Thermal soaring turns the atmosphere into an energy landscape~\cite{Shepard2013}: a bird or a
glider gains height by circling in rising air and spends it gliding to the next
thermal~\cite{Akos2008,Keskin2025}. Thermals are invisible, they form and decay within tens of
minutes, and where they rise is only partly predictable from the ground, so that learning to
find them is a problem of its own~\cite{Reddy2016,Reddy2018}. A cross-country flight is a search,
and its statistics carry the signature of the search: over hours the horizontal motion is
superdiffusive, close to ballistic, with a Hurst exponent near 0.9 for paragliders, hang gliders
and sailplanes alike~\cite{Vilpellet2026}.

Soaring birds use each other to find lift. Vultures informed by others about the next updraft
start their glides faster~\cite{Williams2018}, and vultures flying freely rely on the thermals
others use, more so in poor conditions~\cite{Sassi2024}. In a migrating flock of white storks a
few leaders find and explore the thermals, while followers leave them earlier and lower and flap
more~\cite{Flack2018}. Groups can follow gradients that single individuals cannot
sense~\cite{Berdahl2013}, and pooling the noisy headings of many individuals improves
navigation~\cite{Simons2004,Codling2007,Berdahl2018}. Whether flying together makes soaring flight
more efficient has not been measured, for birds or for pilots. The obstacle is selection: groups
form where and when flying is good, and those who join them are not a random sample of
individuals~\cite{Manski1993}. A raw comparison of group and solo flight mixes the effect of the
group with those of the route, the day and the pilot.

Here we measure it on human pilots. We find the pilots who fly together with a rule calibrated
against chance encounters, and compare group and solo flight three times: as they are; on the same
route, with the same pilots alone as a control; and within one flight, before, during and after
a group. Together they point to one reading: company gives a pilot the route, and on that route it
makes them faster.

\textit{Data and groups.} The French free-flight federation (FFVL) keeps the tracks of the
cross-country flights its pilots declare. We use the \LtFlights{} paraglider flights recorded
between \LtFirstYear{} and \LtLastYear{} with a logger of at most \LtLoggerMax~s. Two pilots are
in contact when one passes where the other passed shortly before, on a similar heading, and the
contact lasts at least $\tau^* = \tauStar$~min. We chose $\tau^*$ as the duration at which
surrogate encounters, made by shifting flights in time, account for at most \LtNullShare\% of the
contacts (Appendix~\ref{app:groups}). A group is a set of pilots linked by contacts, directly or
through other pilots, with the same members over an interval. Then \LtGroupShare\% of the flights fly in a group at some point,
\LtPairShare\% of that time in pairs (Fig.~\ref{fig:design}). We compare three populations: the
group time of these flights (G), the same flights while alone (A) and the flights never in a
group (S).

\textit{Measuring persistence.} Every flight is split into climbs, searches and transitions by the
labelling of Ref.~\cite{Vilpellet2026}. Persistence is read from the time-averaged mean squared
displacement of the horizontal position through its local exponent
\begin{equation}
\Hloc(\tau) = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{d \ln \mathrm{MSD}(\tau)}{d \ln \tau},
\end{equation}
which is $1/2$ for diffusion and 1 for straight flight~\cite{Metzler2014}. The comparisons use the
flights that end away from their takeoff, since closed circuits bend the MSD at long lags [SM]. All
bands are 90\% intervals of a bootstrap over pilots and over episodes and site-days, combined as
in Ref.~\cite{Cameron2011} (Appendix~\ref{app:bands}); on synthetic data with no group effect they
cover zero in \CovNewMin--\CovNewMax\% of the cases.

\textit{Raw comparison.} Below \RawGapCross~s pilots in a group are slightly less persistent than
solo pilots; beyond, they are more persistent (Fig.~\ref{fig:persistence}a,
Table~\ref{tab:persistence}). The crossover is the duration of one leg: the median glide lasts
\RawGlideMedianG~s and the median climb \RawClimbMedianG~s, in G as in S. Over a manoeuvre the group
costs a little; over the succession of thermals that makes a route, it pays. Group time is also
faster: over 30 min it progresses at \RawStatXcG~km/h, against \RawStatXcS~km/h for solo flights.
These gaps mix the group with the route, the day and the pilot.

\textit{Same route.} We matched every piece of group flight to pieces of solo flights along the same
route, in the same season, at the same hour and on a wing of the same class [SM]. On the same route
the gap at 1000~s falls to \MatDeltaHKG{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:persistence}b). The same flights while
alone, matched in the same way, keep part of this gap against their own solo pieces, clearly so at
longer lags [SM]. The effect of the group at fixed route and pilot is the difference of the two, and
it cannot be told from zero, at 1000~s as at longer lags (Table~\ref{tab:persistence}). Speed
behaves otherwise: at fixed route and pilot the group still gains \MatDeltaXcD{}
\MatDeltaXcDBand~km/h of cross-country speed over 30 min, on the pieces long enough to measure it
[SM]. In a wider
match on the route alone, over all flights, fixing the route removes \RouteExplained\% of the gap
between the matched pieces and the solo flights of their site, day and hour [SM].

\textit{Same flight.} Within one flight the pilot and the day are fixed, and the route changes with
the company. In the flights that join a group and later leave it, we cut four windows of
\PreWindow~s: before the group, at its start, at its end and after it [SM]. The changes at entry,
$D_\mathrm{in}$, and at exit, $D_\mathrm{out}$, have a mean $g = (D_\mathrm{in} + D_\mathrm{out})/2$
free of any linear drift along the flight, and a half difference $d_w = (D_\mathrm{in} -
D_\mathrm{out})/2$ that measures this drift. Persistence at 1000~s rises by $g = \PreGHK$ in the
group and returns to its level when the pilot leaves (Fig.~\ref{fig:persistence}c). The drift is
compatible with zero. Mock events, the same four windows placed at the same times of flight in
flights never in a group, show no jump (Table~\ref{tab:persistence}): the course of a flight does
not produce it. Cross-country speed over 30 min rises by \PreGXc~km/h in the group, \PreNetGXc~km/h
net of the mock events [SM]. The band of the effect at fixed route stays below this jump: most of
the jump is the line the group flies.

\textit{Long glides.} The transitions between climbs last longer in group time
(Fig.~\ref{fig:survival}). We compare the tails of their durations through the Hill
estimator~\cite{Hill1975},
\begin{equation}
\hat\mu = n\Bigl/\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ln\frac{x_i}{x_\mathrm{min}},
\end{equation}
of the survival $P(X>x)\sim x^{-\mu}$ over the $n$ durations $x_i$ above $x_\mathrm{min}$. We read
every population at one threshold, $x_\mathrm{min} = \TlXmin$~s, chosen on S by the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance~\cite{Clauset2009}; the bands are those of Appendix~\ref{app:bands} at
fixed threshold, and the index changes with the threshold [SM]. Raw, the tail of group time is
heavier, $\hat\mu = \TlRawMuG$ against \TlRawMuS{} for solo flights (Table~\ref{tab:tail}). The other
two designs do not confirm it. On the same route the effect of the group on $\hat\mu$ cannot be told
from zero, with a wide band. Within the flight, the transitions flown entirely in a group have the
index of those flown before and after it; the heavy tail of group time comes from the transitions
that cross the start or the end of a group [SM]. The long transitions of a group cost less height
per km, and so do those of solo pilots on the same route [SM]: a long, cheap glide belongs to the
line.

\textit{How a group holds a route.} Lift is tied to places: a place where a glider climbed keeps
producing climbs for about \StMkHalfGroup~min, and a glider climbing there now raises the rate of
new climbs by a factor of only \StMkNowGroup{} over one that climbed there five minutes earlier
[SM]. A route is a chain of such places. Groups fly it as files: the pilot ahead changes
\StSwitches{} times per hour, and the lead goes more often to the more experienced pilot and to the
higher wing class [SM]. Followers turn where the leader turned: the correlation of the headings of a
pair peaks at a time lag equal to their delay along the route, at every delay, with no second peak
near zero lag (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}a). A follower retraces the line and does not answer the leader's
turns, and surrogate pairs, who never saw each other, correlate as much at the longer delays [SM].
Groups form mostly as chance meetings on such lines: pairs attract only \StEnSoloPair{} times more
than independent meetings [SM]. On the line the phases of flight change (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}b).
Within the flight, joining a group raises the time in climb and the share of searches that end in a
climb, and glides become faster by \PreGGlide~km/h and flatter by \PreGSink~m per km. At fixed route
and pilot the glides no longer change, while the gain in climb time remains, \MatDeltaClimbD{}
percentage points, together with the gain in speed [SM].

\textit{Terrain.} If the route is what a group gives, the gain should be largest where the route is
hard to find. We classed every point of every flight by the local relief within about 10~km,
following Hammond's landform classes~\cite{Hammond1964} as computed from an elevation
model~\cite{Dikau1991} [SM]. The gain grows with the relief (Fig.~\ref{fig:how}c): raw, from
\LtTerRawLow{} in plains and hills to \LtTerRawHigh{} in high mountains. Within the same flight it
is clear in mountains and not resolved in plains and hills, where few events are available [SM].
Alone, a pilot in plains and hills already flies nearly straight, with $\Hloc(1000~\mathrm{s}) =
\LtTerHlocSLow$ against \LtTerHlocSHigh{} in high mountains, so there is less to gain there; in high
mountains, where the relief bends the route, the group straightens it most.

\textit{Discussion.} Two features of the effect tie it to the route. It appears only beyond one
leg, where the choice of the next thermal matters, and it lasts as long as the group. Faster glides
are what speed-to-fly theory prescribes when the next climb is surer or
stronger~\cite{MacCready1954,Cochrane1999}, and what vultures do when others reveal the next
thermal~\cite{Williams2018}. In storks, followers gain from leaders who find the
thermals~\cite{Flack2018}; among pilots the lead goes to the more experienced, yet followers retrace
the line and do not answer the leader's turns. The long glides tell the same story: their heavier tail and
their lower cost in height come with the line, not with the company. Our data do not fully separate a group that leads pilots to the good line from a
group that gathers on it. Either way, for a pilot the group is where the route is. Comparing one
flight with itself, before, during and after a group, could measure the value of company for
soaring birds tagged in flocks.
