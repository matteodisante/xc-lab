# Reference list for the letter (verified)

Checked 2026-11-09. Bibliographic data (authors, year, volume, pages, DOI) were checked against the Crossref record of each DOI, plus Europe PMC (abstracts), arXiv, the GMBA site and the USGS archive where noted. Page numbers or article numbers not shown by Crossref were checked on Europe PMC/PubMed. Content claims ("Shows") come from abstracts or from the text of open copies. Nothing here is from memory alone; where a primary text could not be opened this is stated.

Format: **key**: citation, DOI, URL. Shows: what it found. Use: sentence it supports. Verdict.

Corrections to the candidate list (details in the entries):

- "Akos, Nagy, Vicsek 2008 PNAS (thermal soaring of birds and UAVs)" is wrong. The 2008 PNAS paper is "Comparing bird and human soaring strategies". "Thermal soaring flight of birds and UAVs" is Akos, Nagy, Leven, Vicsek 2010, Bioinspiration & Biomimetics.
- Williams 2018 title is "Social eavesdropping allows for a more risky gliding strategy by thermal-soaring birds" (Gyps vultures, not "riskier ... in vultures").
- Sherub et al. 2016 is not about storks and not social: it is about Himalayan vultures' thermalling at altitude. Dropped.
- MacCready's "Optimum airspeed selector" is 1954 (Soaring 18(2), Mar-Apr, p. 8), not 1958. The 1958 date appears in the reference list of Akos 2008 and has no match in the Soaring index.
- Harel et al. on vultures and conspecific information: the paper that exists is Harel, Spiegel, Getz, Nathan 2017 (information centre hypothesis, carcass foraging), not about thermals.
- Flack 2018 Science is the leaders/followers paper. The thermalling-specific companion is Nagy, Couzin, Fiedler, Wikelski, Flack 2018, Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B (Nagy is first author).
- Kapos 2000 is a book chapter (CABI), not a journal paper.

## Introduction

  Use: soaring birds and human pilots solve the same thermal-to-thermal problem, and pilot GPS tracks are a valid dataset for it.
  Verdict: keep. The only prior PNAS-level study of human glider tracks; sets the speed-to-fly link.

### Terrain classes from a DEM

All thresholds below were read on the source named. Metric values are as printed; feet-to-metres conversions are noted.

- **Hammond1964**: E. H. Hammond, "Analysis of properties in land form geography: an application to broad-scale land form mapping", Ann. Assoc. Am. Geogr. 54(1), 11-19 (1964). DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8306.1964.tb00470.x. Crossref record checked. The paper is behind a paywall and I could not open it; the thresholds come from Dikau et al. 1991 (USGS Open-File Report 91-634, open PDF), which describes Hammond's manual procedure and quotes Hammond 1964a, p. 17.
  Window: a square window of about 9.65 km (6 mi) per side moved across 1:250,000 maps in non-overlapping steps of 9.65 km.
  Gentle slope: slope under 8%; the share of the window that is gentle gives 4 classes: A more than 80%, B 50-80%, C 20-50%, D less than 20%.
  Local relief (maximum minus minimum elevation in the window): 6 classes: 1: 0-30 m (0-100 ft); 2: 30-91 m (100-300 ft); 3: 91-152 m (300-500 ft); 4: 152-305 m (500-1000 ft); 5: 305-915 m (1000-3000 ft); 6: more than 915 m (3000 ft).
  Profile type: 4 classes by the share of gentle slope lying in lowland or upland (more than 75% lowland; 50-75% lowland; 50-75% upland; more than 75% upland); separates tablelands and plains with hills.
  Class names (Dikau Table 4, "modified slightly from Hammond"): with D (less than 20% gentle): relief 1 very low hills, 2 low hills, 3 moderate hills, 4 high hills, 5 low mountains, 6 high mountains. With C: the same with "open" in front. Plains need A or B (more than 50% gentle) with relief 1 or 2 (flat plains, smooth plains, irregular plains with slight or moderate relief); A or B with relief 3-6 are tablelands or plains with hills/mountains depending on profile. So low mountains = relief 305-915 m and high mountains = relief above 915 m, in terrain with less than 50% gentle slope.
  Not verified: Hammond's own wording of the class names ("very low hills" and "low hills" were added by Dikau according to the USGS report, so the exact relief ranges of Hammond's "hills" are not settled by this source).
  Verdict: keep. The base definition.

- **Dikau1991**: R. Dikau, E. E. Brabb, R. M. Mark, "Landform classification of New Mexico by computer", U.S. Geological Survey Open-File Report 91-634 (1991). DOI 10.3133/ofr91634. https://pubs.usgs.gov/of/1991/0634/report.pdf (read in full).
  Method: DEM at 200 m spacing (from 3 arc-second DMA data). Slope from a 3x3 point window (quadratic surface), then the share of slope under 8% and the local relief (max minus min) in a 49x49 point window = 9.8 km, moved in 200 m steps; thresholds as Hammond above (same slope, relief and profile classes, 96 subclasses). Reports a comparison table: Hammond 9.65 km window and 9.65 km steps; computer version 9.8 km window and 200 m steps.
  Verdict: keep. Gives the pixel-wise (moving window) version of Hammond.

- **Karagulle2017**: D. Karagulle, C. Frye, R. Sayre, S. Breyer, P. Aniello, R. Vaughan, D. Wright, "Modeling global Hammond landform regions from 250-m elevation data", Trans. GIS 21(5), 1040-1060 (2017). DOI 10.1111/tgis.12265. Abstract read at https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70193965. The full text (Wiley) returned 403; I could not open it.
  From the abstract: 250 m global DEM; adds a profile parameter to delineate tablelands; handles negative elevations; lets the neighbourhood analysis window (NAW) size differ between parameters; maps regions, not pixels; improves on the USGS global Hammond features product, which used one fixed NAW.
  Thresholds and windows, from the documentation of Esri's implementation (arcgis.com item cd817a746aa7437cbd72a6d39cdb4559, "World Ecological Facets Landform Classes", which states the algorithm is "published and described by Karagulle et al. 2017", run on 250 m GMTED2010): slope = share of gentle terrain (Hammond's 8% threshold) in a 3 km neighbourhood, 4 classes 0-20%, 21-50%, 51-80%, more than 80% (class ids 400, 300, 200, 100). Local relief (max minus min) in a 6 km neighbourhood, 6 classes: 0-30, 31-90, 91-150, 151-300, 301-900, more than 900 m. Profile in a 6 km circular neighbourhood (a 6 km radius in the Esri blog, not confirmed in the item text), 5 classes. Result: 16 classes, four of them mountain classes (high mountains, low mountains, scattered high mountains, scattered low mountains), plains overriding "plains with hills/mountains" where the area is exclusively plains.
  Not verified: the window sizes and metric thresholds as printed in the paper itself (the Esri text rounds Hammond's feet to 90, 150, 300, 900 m, where Dikau uses 91, 152, 305, 915 m).
  Verdict: keep, with the caveat. Cite the Esri-layer thresholds only after the paper's table is checked.

- **Meybeck2001**: M. Meybeck, P. Green, C. J. Vorosmarty, "A new typology for mountains and other relief classes: an application to global continental water resources and population distribution", Mt. Res. Dev. 21(1), 34-45 (2001). DOI 10.1659/0276-4741(2001)021[0034:ANTFMA]2.0.CO;2. Read in full in the open PDF at https://www.geobotany.uaf.edu/library/pubs/MaybeckM2001_mrd_34-45.pdf (the BioOne page itself blocked me).
  Relief roughness: RR = (maximum minus minimum elevation, from 1 km GTOPO30, in a 30 arc-minute x 30 arc-minute cell) / (half the cell length between cell centres, depending on latitude and flow direction); units m/km = per mille. Range from under 1 to 399 per mille. (My arithmetic: half a 30' cell is about 28 km, so RR 20 per mille is about 550 m of elevation range in a cell; this is approximate and latitude-dependent.)
  RR classes (geometric): under 5 (subhorizontal), 5-10, 10-20, 20-40 (poorly dissected), 40-80, 80-160, over 160.
  Combined with mean cell elevation (0-200, 200-500, 500-1000, then every 1000 m). The 15 relief classes (Table 2): 1-3 plains (RR under 5; mean elevation 0-200, 200-500, over 500 m); 4 lowlands (0-200 m, RR 5-20); 5 rugged lowlands (0-200 m, RR 20-80); 6 platforms (200-500 m, RR 5-20); 7-10 plateaus (RR 5-40, mean elevation 500-1000, 1000-2000, 2000-4000, over 4000 m); 11 hills (200-500 m, RR 20 to 160); 12 low mountains (mean elevation 500-1000 m, RR above 20); 13 mid-altitude mountains (1000-2000 m, RR above 20); 14 high mountains (2000-4000 m, RR above 40); 15 very high mountains (4000-6000 m, RR above 40). Rule in the text: mountains need mean elevation above 500 m and RR above 20 per mille at 500-2000 m, above 40 per mille at 2000-6000 m. Tibet and the Altiplano are plateaus.
  Caveat for a 1-km or 90 m use: the thresholds are tied to a 30' cell; they are not scale-free.
  Verdict: keep. Gives the plains / hills / low / high mountains vocabulary with explicit numbers. Note it also gives its own (second-hand) summary of Kapos with a 5 km radius, which conflicts with the 7 km given by Kapos-based sources below.
