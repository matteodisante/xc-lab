\section{Open flights}\label{sm:open}

Which flights do the comparisons of the letter use? The declared open flights whose opening is above
\LtOpenThreshold; this section says why.

The archive of the French free-flight federation (FFVL) holds \LtFlightsAll{} paraglider flights
recorded between \LtFirstYear{} and \LtLastYear. We use the \LtOpenTotal{} flights that depart and
are logged at most every \LtLoggerMax~s.

The pilot declares the type of each flight. Of these flights \LtOpenDeclared{} are declared open (a
free distance or a route with up to three turn points), \FlightsClosed{} closed (a triangle, a
quadrilateral or an out-and-return) and \FlightsNeither{} neither. \pending{State how the tracks are cleaned (spikes, gaps, altitude); the tracks are those cleaned by
soaring-anomalous-transport (tag legacy-final), and the rule is in its preprocessing.}

The opening of a flight is the distance from its takeoff to its landing over the path flown. It is 1
for a straight line and close to 0 for a flight that returns to its takeoff. Declared open flights
are often folded: their median opening is \MedianEvery{}, and \BelowEvery\% of them have an opening
below \LtOpenThreshold{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_opening}).

A flight that returns near its takeoff moves away and then back, so its displacement stops growing as
the lag approaches its duration and its mean squared displacement (MSD) bends. Closed circuits
therefore bend the MSD at long lags. On the dataset to \DeclTauLongest~s (Sec.~S2) the local slope
$\alpha$ at that lag falls to \LtDeclLongestAlphaS{} for solo flights (S) and
\LtDeclLongestAlphaA{} for the same flights alone (A).

Group time (G) keeps \LtDeclLongestAlphaG{} on \LtDeclFlightsG{} flights only. The flights of G are
the most open at these lags (Table~\ref{tab:opening}), so part of the straighter progress of G at
long lags is the shape of its flights.

We keep the declared open flights with an opening above \LtOpenThreshold, \LtOpenKept{} flights or
\LtOpenKeptShare\% of the declared open ones. We chose \LtOpenThreshold{} as the smallest selection
that removes the flights that return near their takeoff, and that keeps similar shares of the flights
from low and high takeoffs, \TakeoffLowKeep\% below and \TakeoffHighKeep\% above \MountainM~m.

A stricter threshold of \Stricter{} keeps only \TakeoffLowStrict\% and \TakeoffHighStrict\% of them.
It would select the region as well as the shape, and a high threshold selects straight flights and
raises the exponent by construction.

\section{Datasets and the $\tau/\LtSupport$ rule}\label{sm:datasets}

Why does the main dataset stop at \LtTauTwo~s, and what does the choice of the dataset change? A
lag needs long enough pieces, and longer pieces are fewer and differ from the short ones.

A lag $\tau$ needs pieces of at least $\tau/\LtSupport$~s, so that the origins of every piece cover the
lag in the same share of it. A dataset to the lag $T$ keeps in each population the pieces of at least
$T/\LtSupport$~s and measures them at every lag up to $T$, so that the population does not change along
a curve.

We use three datasets. The main dataset has $T = \RawTauTwo$~s and pieces of at least
\RawShortestTwo~s. The check has $T = \RawTauOne$~s and pieces of at least \RawShortestOne~s. The longest,
to \LtTauLong~s, shows what longer pieces select. Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_datasets} shows their MSD, their
$\alpha(\tau)$, their fitted $H$ and their number of flights, and Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_durations} the
durations of the pieces.

The value $\aK$ is read on the datasets to \LtTauOne~s, where its window is cut at \LtLagOne~s, and on
the others, where it is not.

$\aQ$ is read on the datasets to \LtTauTwo~s and \LtTauLong~s. On the first its window of
$\pm\LtDecade$ decades would run from \HqLowest{} to \HqHighest~s and is cut at \RawTauTwo~s.

Longer pieces select flights with a larger exponent. On the dataset to \LtTauLong~s $\aK$ is
\LtRawAlphaKSLong{} for S, \LtRawAlphaKALong{} for A and \LtRawAlphaKGLong{} for G, larger than on the
main dataset (Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_datasets}). The level shifts. The shape of $\alpha(\tau)$ at the lags
that the datasets share is kept.

We chose \LtTauTwo~s as the longest lag at which the same-route design keeps \MatMin{} pieces of G
per level of the wing. Table~\ref{tab:counts} gives the pieces of G and A that find a control for each
caliper (Sec.~S6) on the datasets to \LtTauOne{} and \LtTauTwo~s.

At $T = \LtLongerTau$~s no caliper reaches \LtMinPieces{} pieces per level: the most beginners any
caliper keeps is \LtLongerBeginners. We counted this once, outside the final run, with the matching of
Sec.~S6.

\section{Estimate of $\alpha$ and of $H$}\label{sm:estimate}

How do we read persistence from the MSD? Through the local slope $\alpha(\tau)$ and through the
exponent $H$ fitted over the whole curve.

For a flight $f$ and a population, the time-averaged MSD pools the origins of all its pieces in that
population,
\begin{equation}
\overline{\delta^2_f}(\tau) = \frac{1}{N_f(\tau)} \sum_t \bigl\| \mathbf{r}(t+\tau) - \mathbf{r}(t) \bigr\|^2 ,
\end{equation}
with $t$ and $t+\tau$ in the same piece, on a \TnGridStep~s grid. The ensemble $\mathrm{MSD}(\tau)$ is
the mean over the flights, with one weight per flight (per piece, control segment or window in the
other designs).

The local slope is
\begin{equation}
\alpha(\tau) = \frac{d \ln \mathrm{MSD}}{d \ln \tau},
\end{equation}
estimated as the slope of $\ln\mathrm{MSD}$ against $\ln\tau$ over the lags within $\pm\LtDecade$
decades of $\tau$, with the three nearest lags when fewer lie there. At the last lag of a dataset the
window is one-sided. $\alpha$ is 1 for diffusion and 2 for straight flight.

The exponent $H$ is half the slope of $\ln\mathrm{MSD}$ against $\ln\tau$ from \LtFitFrom~s to the end
lag, with the same weight for every lag; for a power law $\mathrm{MSD}\sim\tau^{2H}$, $\alpha = 2H$.
Because $H$ averages $\alpha$ over the lags of the fit, it depends on the end lag when $\alpha$ is not
constant. The gap of G against S in the fitted $H$ grows with the end lag
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_fit_by_end}b).

\section{Groups}\label{sm:groups}

Which flights are in a group? Appendix~A of the letter gives the identification rule and this section
its parameters and the three populations.

Table~\ref{tab:groups} lists the parameters. Contacts are sought among all flights of the archive, so
a pilot may meet any other pilot whose track was uploaded. Only uploaded tracks are seen, and a group
may have more members than we count.

With these values \LtGroupFlights{} flights (\LtGroupShare\% of the eligible flights) fly in a group
at some point, for \LtGroupHours{} flight-hours, and groups of two pilots hold \LtPairShare\% of this
time.

A flight is split into stretches on its own clock. After its departure, every instant of a flight that
entered a group at least once is in a group or alone.

We compare three populations: G, the stretches in a group of the flights that entered one; A, the other
stretches of those flights, flown by the same pilots on the same gliders and days; and S, the flights
that never entered a group. A piece is a stretch within one segment of the track, on a \TnGridStep~s
grid of the track clock.

\section{Uncertainty bands}\label{sm:bands}

Do the bands of the letter mean what they say? We checked their coverage on synthetic data.

Appendix~B of the letter describes the two-way bootstrap of pilots and blocks~\cite{Cameron2011}. We
use \NBoot{} draws of pilots, of blocks and of cells, and the same draws for all the quantities of a
design, so that a difference of two quantities has the band of its own draws.

We checked the coverage on synthetic data that keep the real flights, pilots, blocks, pieces and
events of the three designs and carry no group effect, in \CovReplicates{} replicates of several mixes
of the variances of pilots, blocks, flights and units, with \CovDraws{} draws per bootstrap
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_coverage}).

A \LtBandLevel\% band holds the true value in \CovNewMin--\CovNewMax\% of the replicates. A bootstrap
over blocks of episodes alone, without pilots, holds it in only \CovOldRawMin\% of the replicates when
pilots matter.

The standard error of the band is \CovNewSdMin{} to \CovNewSdMax{} times the standard deviation of the
estimate over the replicates. A band that excludes 0 is a two-sided test at \LtBandLevel\%, so among
many quantities without an effect about one in ten excludes 0 by chance. The bands do not include the
choice of the matching rules, which Sec.~S12 varies, nor the choice of the threshold of the Hill
estimator.

\emph{Matching.} For each piece of G, the controls are stretches of flights of S on another date. A
control matches the piece when a share \MatCover{} of its points, taken every \LtMatchStep~s, lies within
\MatRho~m of the control track and the control flew them in the same direction. The control stretch
runs between the points of its track nearest to the first and to the last of these points, and
lasts at least as long as the dataset needs. Each piece may have several controls, which share its
weight.

A caliper then keeps the controls of the same season (month within \MatMonth), the same hour
(within \MatHour{} hours, local time of the middle of the piece and of the stretch) and a wing of the
same class. The pool holds \MatPool{} flights of S and gives \MatMatches{} matches before the
caliper. We chose the caliper by trying the narrower ones first and taking the first that keeps at
least \MatMin{} pieces of G per level of the wing on the dataset to \LtTauTwo~s (Table~\ref{tab:counts}):
month within \MatMonth, hour within \MatHour, same class.

Table~\ref{tab:counts} gives the pieces kept on each dataset. On the dataset to \LtTauOne~s the pieces
of G come from \LtRouteFlightsGOne{} flights and \LtRoutePilotsGOne{} pilots, and those of A from
\LtRouteFlightsAOne{} flights and \LtRoutePilotsAOne{} pilots. We write S$|$G and S$|$A for the
controls of the pieces of G and of A.

The pieces of G have \LtRouteControlsGOne{} control stretches and those of A \LtRouteControlsAOne{} on
the dataset to \LtTauOne~s, and \LtRouteControlsGTwo{} and \LtRouteControlsATwo{} on the dataset to
\LtTauTwo~s.

\emph{Selection and the effect of the group.} The contrast A $-$ S$|$A repeats the comparison for the
flights that join groups, taken while they fly alone, and measures the selection of these flights. In
both contrasts the solo pieces come from other flights, never in a group, that share only the route.

The two sides are not paired, so the difference removes selection at the level of the two populations
and no pilot is compared with themselves. The effect of the group on the same route, net of
selection, is the difference (G $-$ S$|$G) $-$ (A $-$ S$|$A). Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_route_forest} and
Table~\ref{tab:designs} give the values at \LtLagOne~s and \LtLagTwo~s.

On the main dataset the gap of G against its controls is no smaller than the raw gap. The same flights
alone differ from their controls by a band that excludes 0 at \LtLagTwo~s and is of the size of the gap
of G. The effect net of selection is \LtRouteAlphaKDiffTwo{} \LtRouteAlphaKDiffTwoBand{} at
\LtLagOne~s and \LtRouteAlphaQDiffTwo{} \LtRouteAlphaQDiffTwoBand{} at \LtLagTwo~s: it is not resolved,
and its band includes values as large as the raw gap.

On the check dataset the gap of G is about half the raw gap, and the difference is
\LtRouteAlphaKDiffOne{} \LtRouteAlphaKDiffOneBand. The fitted $H$ gives a difference of
\LtRouteFitDiffOne{} \LtRouteFitDiffOneBand{} on the check dataset, resolved and of the opposite sign
to the gap, and \LtRouteFitDiffTwo{} \LtRouteFitDiffTwoBand{} on the main one.

The three contrasts show that the same route keeps the gap of G, that the flights that join groups
keep part of it, and that the design does not separate the group from this selection for persistence.

\emph{Route alone.} A second match tests how much of the gap the route explains. It takes the flights
of S that never had a contact of any length (\RouteOpenAlone{} flights), and matches a piece of G to a
flight from the same site on another day in the same way. It uses all \RouteOpenSample{} pieces of G
of the dataset to \LtTauOne~s, of which \RouteOpenPieces{} find at least one match.

The matched pieces come from \RouteOpenFlights{} flights and \RouteOpenPilots{} pilots, against
\RouteOpenControlFlights{} control flights of \RouteOpenControlPilots{} pilots. The baseline compares
the same pieces with the solo flights of their site, day and hour.

Fixing the route lowers the gap at \LtLagOne~s from \LtRouteOpenBaseAlpha{}
\LtRouteOpenBaseAlphaBand{} to \LtRouteOpenAlpha{} \LtRouteOpenAlphaBand, so that the route removes
\RouteOpenExplained\% (\RouteOpenExplainedBand) of the gap to the flights of the same site, day and
hour. This share rests on this sample and this baseline only. It is not the gap against the same-route
solo pieces of the matched design, and we do not quote it in the letter.

\section{Same-flight design}\label{sm:flight}

Does persistence change within a flight when the pilot joins a group? This section gives the windows,
the estimator $g$ and the mock events that test it.

\emph{Windows.} For each stretch of a flight in a group we cut four windows on the clock of the flight.
Pre is the window before the stretch, if the flight was alone for at least that long. Start and end
are its first and last windows, if the stretch lasts that long. Post is the window after it, if the
flight is alone for that long. A set of four windows is one event. The MSD is measured on the lags of
the grid up to the largest within $\LtSupport$ times the window: \PreMaxLag~s for windows of
\LtWinMid~s, \LtTauTwo~s for windows of \LtWinMax~s.

The changes at entry and at exit are
$D_\mathrm{in} = \alpha^\mathrm{start} - \alpha^\mathrm{pre}$ and $D_\mathrm{out} =
\alpha^\mathrm{end} - \alpha^\mathrm{post}$, and
\begin{equation}
g = \frac{D_\mathrm{in} + D_\mathrm{out}}{2}, \qquad d_w = \frac{D_\mathrm{in} - D_\mathrm{out}}{2}
\end{equation}
are the effect of the group and the drift over one window. If the quantity drifts linearly with the
flight clock, $D_\mathrm{in}$ is the effect plus the drift and $D_\mathrm{out}$ the effect minus it,
so that $g$ is free of the drift. A profile with curvature along the flight is not removed. If
persistence were low after takeoff and before landing and high between, $g$ would be positive with no
effect of the group. We test this with two controls.

\emph{Controls.} The control pairs are two consecutive windows of \LtWinMid~s drawn at random in solo
time, in the same flights alone (\LtFltMidEventsCtrlA{} events) and in flights of S
(\LtFltMidEventsCtrlS{} events); the change is the later window minus the earlier. At \LtLagOne~s they
give \LtFltMidCtrlAAlphaK{} and \LtFltMidCtrlSAlphaK{}, so a linear drift is small in $\alpha$.

The mock events place the four windows of a real event at the same times of the flight clock in flights
of S. For each of the real events we draw at random up to \PreMockK{} flights of S in which the four
windows fit in one solo stretch at these times, within the caliper of the same-route design
(\PreMockCaliper). We measure and compare them like the real windows. A mock event takes the
resampling cell of its real event, and the mocks of one event weigh one event.

For windows of \LtWinMid~s this gives \LtFltMidMockEvents{} mock events in \LtFltMidMockFlights{}
flights, and for windows of \LtWinMax~s \LtFltMaxMockEvents{} mock events in \LtFltMaxMockFlights{}
flights. Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_windows} gives the real events with a mock. Since these real events are a
subset, $g$ minus mock compares the mock events with them.

In a second variant the mock flight also took off at the site of the real one: \LtFltMidMockSiteEvents{}
mock events for windows of \LtWinMid~s and \LtFltMaxMockSiteEvents{} for windows of \LtWinMax~s.

\emph{Length of the window.} Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_windows} gives the changes for the five windows we cut,
and Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_flight_forest} the changes by quantity for windows of \LtWinMax{} and \LtWinMid~s.

The window of \LtWinShort~s is not a clean check. At \LtLagOne~s the window is \LtWinShortShare{} of its
length, close to the limit of the $\tau/\LtSupport$ rule, \LtLagOne~s is the last lag of its MSD so
that its estimate is one-sided, and $D_\mathrm{in}$ is \LtFltShortInAlphaK{} while $D_\mathrm{out}$ is
\LtFltShortOutAlphaK{}, with a drift $d_w$ of \LtFltShortDwAlphaK{}. The $\alpha$ at \LtLagOne~s is not
comparable there.

At \LtLagTwo~s, which the windows of \LtWinMid~s and longer allow, $g$ is \LtFltMidGAlphaQ{}
\LtFltMidGAlphaQBand{} with \LtWinMid~s, \LtFltLongGAlphaQ{} \LtFltLongGAlphaQBand{} with
\LtWinLong~s and \LtFltMaxGAlphaQ{} \LtFltMaxGAlphaQBand{} with \LtWinMax~s. The bands overlap and the
events differ, so we do not say that the windows differ.

\emph{Mock events.} The mock events show a small change at \LtLagTwo~s and in speed with windows of

\section{Phases and cross-country speed}\label{sm:phases}

Which phases of flight change with the group, and does the speed? The letter reports the speed and the
phase statistics net of the mock events; this section defines them.

\emph{Phases.} Every flight logged at 1~Hz is cut into climbs, searches and transitions by the
labelling of Ref.~\cite{Vilpellet2026}. A climb is a stretch of circling in which the glider gains
height, a transition a straight glide between two climbs, and a search a stretch flown with turns and
without a sustained gain.

Phases exist on the flights logged at 1~Hz, so the phase statistics rest on fewer flights than the MSD
(Fig.~\ref{fig:sm_datasets}): in the raw comparison \RawPhaseFlightsS{} flights of S,
\RawPhaseFlightsA{} of A and \RawPhaseFlightsG{} of G.

We use four statistics: the time in climb, the share of searches that end in a climb (a search ends in
a climb when the next phase of the flight is a climb), the glide speed (the straight-line distance of
the transitions over their duration, in km/h) and the height lost per km of glide (m/km). A phase
belongs to the unit that holds its middle. The unit is the flight in the raw design, the piece or the
control segment in the same-route design and the window in the same-flight design.

For a unit $u$ let $n_u$ and $d_u$ be the numerator and the denominator of the statistic and $T_u$ its
phase time. The statistic of a population is the ratio of averages
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sum_u w_u\, n_u/T_u}{\sum_u w_u\, d_u/T_u},
\end{equation}
with $w_u$ the weight of the unit (one, or the bootstrap count in a draw). For the time in climb $n_u$
is the time in climbs and $d_u = T_u$. We average before we divide so that a unit with a single search
does not give 0\% or 100\%, and no long flight dominates.

\emph{Cross-country speed.} In a piece, a control stretch or a window, the speed is the mean over the
origins $t$ on the grid of the straight-line distance $\|\mathbf{r}(t+\LtXcMin~\mathrm{min}) -
\mathbf{r}(t)\|$ divided by \LtXcMin~min, in km/h. It needs pieces of at least $\LtXcS/\LtSupport$~s.

Raw, the speed is \LtRawXcS{} \LtRawXcSBand{} km/h for S, \LtRawXcA{} \LtRawXcABand{} for A and
\LtRawXcG{} \LtRawXcGBand{} for G. Table~\ref{tab:designs} gives the gaps and the samples of the three
designs. On the same route the same flights alone go as fast as their controls, and the effect of the
group net of selection is the row difference of that table.

In the same flight, Table~\ref{tab:designs} gives $g$, the drift $d_w$, the mock events and $g$ minus
mock for windows of \LtWinMax~s. With windows of \LtWinMid~s the mock events give \PreMockGXc{}
\PreMockGXcBand~km/h, and Table~\ref{tab:speed} gives $g$ and $g$ minus mock.

\emph{Phase statistics.} Raw, a search ends in a climb \RawStatFoundG\% of the time in G against
\RawStatFoundS\% in S. On the same route, net of selection, the time in climb grows and the glide
speed and the height lost per km are not resolved (Table~\ref{tab:speed}).

On the same route G glides slower and steeper than S$|$G (\MatDeltaGlideG{} \MatDeltaGlideGBand~km/h and
\MatDeltaSinkG{} \MatDeltaSinkGBand~m/km), and A does too (\MatDeltaGlideA{} \MatDeltaGlideABand~km/h
and \MatDeltaSinkA{} \MatDeltaSinkABand~m/km), so that the effect net of selection is the one of
Table~\ref{tab:speed}.

Within a flight, with windows of \LtWinMid~s, a pilot in a group glides faster and flatter than before
and after (Table~\ref{tab:speed}). The mock events are not null for these statistics:
\PreMockGClimb{} \PreMockGClimbBand{} percentage points of time in climb and \PreMockGGlide{}
\PreMockGGlideBand~km/h of glide speed. Net of the mock events the climbs per hour rise by
\PreNetGCph{} \PreNetGCphBand{} and the climb rate by \PreNetGRate{} \PreNetGRateBand~m/s, which is not
resolved.

\section{Tail of the transitions}\label{sm:tails}

Do long glides change with the group? Raw, the durations of the transitions have a heavier tail in
group time, but the two other designs do not confirm it, and the letter does not use it. This
section gives the Hill index in the three designs.

\emph{Method.} The Hill estimator gives the index $\hat\mu$ of the durations above a threshold
$x_\mathrm{min}$. Following Ref.~\cite{Clauset2009} we take the $x_\mathrm{min}$ that minimises the
Kolmogorov--Smirnov distance between the data above it and the fitted law. We choose it on the solo
flights of the raw design, \TlXmin~s, and read every population of the three designs at this one value.
The bands do not include the uncertainty of the threshold (Fig.~\ref{fig:tail}b).

\emph{Result.} Raw, the tail of G is heavier than that of S (a lower index), and the gap depends on where
the threshold is put. On the same route the effect of the group is not resolved. In the same flight we class every
transition by the stretch of group time that holds it: the transitions flown entirely in a group have
$\hat\mu = \TlFlightMuIn$, and their difference from the mean of the transitions flown before and after the
group, \TlFlightDeltaIn{} \TlFlightDeltaInBand, is not resolved. The transitions that cross the start or the end of a group have
$\hat\mu = \TlFlightMuSpanning$ \TlFlightMuSpanningBand, so the heavy tail comes from them.

\section{Terrain}\label{sm:terrain}

Is the gain larger where the route is hard to find? The letter reports the gap by terrain class; this
section defines the classes and gives the values.

We class the terrain by the landforms of Hammond~\cite{Hammond1964} in their computer
version~\cite{Dikau1991}. The elevation is the model BD ALTI of the French national geographic
institute (IGN) at \TnSource~m, averaged over blocks of $\TnBlock\times\TnBlock$ cells onto a grid of
\TnCell~m. A block with more than half of its cells without data has no elevation, and neither has a
block whose cells all stand at 0~m, which is sea (\TnMaskedShare\% of the blocks with an elevation).

The slope is taken by central differences on this grid, and a cell is gentle when its slope is below
\TnGentle{} (rise over run). In a square window of $\TnWindow\times\TnWindow$ cells (\TnWindowKm~km)
around a cell we take the relief, the maximum minus the minimum elevation, and the gentle share. A
window with fewer than \TnCoverage{} of valid cells gives the class unknown.

Otherwise the cell is plains if the relief is below \TnPlainsRelief~m and the gentle share above
\TnPlainsGentle, hills if the relief is below \TnHillsRelief~m and it is not plains, low mountains from
\TnHillsRelief{} to \TnLowRelief~m and high mountains from \TnLowRelief~m up.

A piece takes the class of the cell under its grid points when at least \TnMajority\% of them are in
it, and is mixed otherwise (\TnPiecesMixedG{} pieces of G), or unknown when its points have no class
(\TnPiecesUnknownG). An event of the same-flight design takes a class when its four windows share it.

Plains hold few pieces (\TnPiecesPlainsG{} pieces of G against \TnPiecesHillsG{} in the hills), so we
join plains and hills as lowland. Of the grid points of G, \TnShareLowlandG\% lie over the lowland and
\TnShareMountainG\% over the mountains, against \TnShareLowlandS\% and \TnShareMountainS\% for S.

Table~\ref{tab:terrain} gives $\Delta\aK$ by class. Raw, the gap is alike in the lowland and in the low
mountains, and several times larger in the high mountains: a step at the high mountains. Alone, a pilot
over the lowland already flies nearly straight, $\aK = \LtTnAlphaSLowland$ against \LtTnAlphaSHigh{}
over high mountains, so that there is less to gain there.

Few pieces of G over the lowland find a control on the same route (Table~\ref{tab:terrain}):
\TnRouteMatchedShareLowland\% of them, against \TnRouteMatchedShareMountain\% over the mountains. We
did not test why. The class comes from the terrain under the piece, and the pieces over the mountains
come from many more pilots (Table~\ref{tab:terrain}), who also differ in sites and in weather. We read
three classes and two designs at once and make no correction for it.

How does a group hold a route? The letter reports the order in a group, the headings, the marking of
places and the encounters; this section defines them.

\emph{Order.} In a group, the pilot ahead is the one that passes a place first. In groups of three or
more, we count the share of the time in which one pilot is ahead, when the ordering is acyclic, and the
changes of the pilot ahead per hour of contact. Table~\ref{tab:structure} compares them with the values
when the directions of the links are drawn at random and with chance contacts of the same duration: the
lead changes less often than by chance.

In groups of two pilots with a known record we count the pairs in which the leader has more earlier
flights in the catalogue (\StExpPairs{} pairs) and the pairs in which the leader has the higher wing
class (\StWingPairs{} pairs). Table~\ref{tab:structure} gives the shares.

\emph{Headings.} In glide, with $\theta_i(t)$ the heading of pilot $i$ and $\theta_\mathrm{route}$ the
direction of the displacement of the centroid over the glide, let $\varphi_i = \theta_i -
\theta_\mathrm{route}$ wrapped to $(-\pi,\pi]$. For a pair with $i$ ahead,
\begin{equation}
C(\tau) = \operatorname{corr}_t\bigl(\varphi_i(t),\,\varphi_j(t+\tau)\bigr),
\end{equation}
over the instants of the glide where both exist, with $\tau > 0$ when $j$ follows $i$. A follower that
retraces the track of the leader peaks at $\tau$ equal to the delay $\ell$ along the route. A follower
that answers the turns of the leader peaks near $\tau = 0$ at every delay.

For delays of \StHdLOne, \StHdLTwo, \StHdLThree{} and \StHdLFour~s (median per class) the peaks of the
pairs lie at \StHdPeakOne, \StHdPeakTwo, \StHdPeakThree{} and \StHdPeakFour~s, close to the delay in
each class though the peak of the longest class comes early. There is no second peak near zero lag in
the classes of \StHdLTwo~s and more. The null model is made of surrogate pairs, two pilots who never
saw each other (\StHdChance{} against \StHdGlides{} pair-glides in groups).

\emph{Marking of places.} The start of a climb is marked when a glider outside the pilot's group is in
a climb within $R_\mathrm{m} = \StMkR$~m of it in the window of $T_\mathrm{m} = \StMkDT$~s before it.
Both are the \StMkPercentile th percentiles of the distance and of the delay between the entries of two
members of one thermal.

Places keep producing climbs, so we shift the markers in time by $\pm s$ and call $m(s)$ the share of
climb starts that are marked. We read $s_{1/2}$, the shift at which $m$ falls to half of $m(0)$, and
the ratio $m(0)/m(s_\mathrm{n})$ with $s_\mathrm{n} = \StMkNear$~min, the average of the shifts of
$+s_\mathrm{n}$ and $-s_\mathrm{n}$, which says how much a glider climbing right then adds over one
that climbed there minutes before or after. The denominator of $m$ counts all climb starts, so
$s_{1/2}$ is a lower bound of how long a place keeps being used for climbs.

\emph{Encounters.} Two gliders not in contact that come close join when they enter a contact. A group
offers more gliders to meet, so it is joined more often without any attraction. With $p(d)$ the share
joined by two solo gliders at the closest distance $d$ and $nm$ the number of pairs of gliders of the
two units, the expected number of joins is
\begin{equation}
E = \sum_\mathrm{passes} \Bigl[ 1 - \bigl(1 - p(d_\mathrm{min})\bigr)^{nm} \Bigr] ,
\end{equation}
and we read the observed joins over $E$.

A solo glider that meets a pair joins it more often than expected, and one that meets a group of three
or more as often as expected (Table~\ref{tab:structure}). This says how pairs attract a third glider
and nothing on how groups form.


What we keep: followers retrace the line, places stay marked for at least \StMkHalfGroup~min, and a
glider climbing right then adds a factor of only \StMkNowGroup.

\section{Robustness}\label{sm:robust}

Does the same-route result depend on the choices of the match? We varied them.

On the dataset to \LtTauOne~s, $\Delta\aK$ stays between \LtRobLowK{} and \LtRobHighK{} over
\LtRobLinesK{} lines, with a band that includes 0 in \LtRobZeroK{} of them.

On the dataset to \LtTauTwo~s, on the \LtRobLinesQ{} lines whose long series reaches \LtLagTwo~s,
$\Delta\aQ$ stays between \LtRobLowQ{} and \LtRobHighQ, with a band that includes 0 in \LtRobZeroQ.
The lines keep between \LtRobPiecesLow{} and \LtRobPiecesHigh{} pieces.
