\section{Calibration: what chance means}\label{sec:calibration}

Two pilots who never interact still meet: they take off from the same site, follow the same
ridges and valleys and climb in the same house thermals. A contact therefore says little by
itself; what counts is how often contacts of a given duration occur beyond chance. Interaction
indices based on standard tests overestimate the interactions with finely sampled
telemetry~\cite{long2014}, so chance is built from the data, with the null model of this section,
and the minimum duration $\tau^*$ follows from it. The run reported here and in the next two
sections covers the 162\,485 cleaned flights of 6\,209 dates of the FFVL archive
(Section~\ref{sec:data}).

\subsection{The null model}\label{sec:null}

On each date, up to 200 pairs $(i,j)$ of flights whose takeoffs are less than $R_s=10$~km apart
are drawn; in each, flight $j$ is replaced by its copy shifted in time:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shift}
  \x^s_j(t)=\x_j(t+r_{ij}),\quad z^s_j(t)=z_j(t+r_{ij}),\qquad
  r_{ij}=(k^0_j-k^0_i)-\vartheta,\quad \vartheta\sim\hat P\bigl(k^0_j-k^0_i\bigr).
\end{equation}
The variable $\vartheta$ is drawn from the delays between the takeoffs of the pairs of that date
with takeoffs within $R_s$, so the copy of $j$ takes off $\vartheta$ after $i$, as a real pilot of
the day could. Only shifts
with $|r_{ij}|\ge r_{\min}=2\delta+\Delta=245$~s are accepted, rounded to the grid step. A pair
enters once, with the roles of $i$ and $j$ drawn at random. The idea of shifting tracks in time,
which keeps the spatial constraints and destroys the synchronisation, comes from Spiegel et
al.~\cite{spiegel2016}, who shuffle whole days of each individual; here a flight is shifted within
its day, against one other flight.

No real correspondence survives the shift. If $(t,t')$ was a real correspondence, in the
surrogate pair the position of $j$ at instant $t'$ appears at instant $t'-r_{ij}$, which is at
least $|r_{ij}|-\delta>\delta$ away from $t$ (Figure~\ref{fig:null}). The correspondences that
remain are encounters on the same route without synchrony.

What the shifted pair keeps: the route, the terrain, the date, and the takeoff delays of the
day as a distribution. What it destroys: every correspondence within $\delta$, since
$|r_{ij}|>2\delta$. On the archive the shift is far larger than its minimum
(Figure~\ref{fig:shifts}): 58~min in the median, and 90\% of the shifts lie between 8~min and
4.4~h. So the copy also flies in the weather of another hour, with other thermals working and
another cloud base.

Chance is therefore an operational notion: the contacts that the same routes give without
synchrony. Two consequences follow.
\begin{itemize}
\item Pilots who use the same thermals at the same time without following each other are in
  synchrony with the weather of the hour, which the shift removes: their contacts count as real.
\item A pilot who follows another deliberately, but more than $\delta$ behind, matches the
  other only beyond $\delta$: the real pair never shows this contact, while a surrogate whose
  shift brings that delay within $\delta$ counts it as chance.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The false discovery rate and \texorpdfstring{$\tau^*$}{tau*}}\label{sec:fdr}

The surrogate pairs go through Phases~1--3 like the real ones (Section~\ref{sec:run}), and their
intervals are the contacts that chance alone gives. Since the surrogates are a sample of the
pairs of each date, their rate is taken date by date. On each date $d$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathcal{P}_d$ is the set of the pairs $(a,b)$, $a<b$, of flights of the date with
  takeoffs less than $R_s$ apart.
\item The exposure of a pair is the time both fly after departure,
  $h_{ab}=\max\bigl(\min(k^\fin_a,k^\fin_b)-\max(k^\dep_a,k^\dep_b),\,0\bigr)$, and
  $H_d=\sum_{\mathcal{P}_d}h_{ab}$.
\item $\mathcal{S}_d$ is the set of the surrogate pairs $(i,j,r)$; $S_d$ is their exposure, the
  same sum with $j$ shifted:
  $S_d=\sum_{\mathcal{S}_d}\max\bigl(\min(k^\fin_i,k^\fin_j-r)-\max(k^\dep_i,k^\dep_j-r),\,0\bigr)$.
\item $M_d(\tau)$ is the number of contact intervals of the surrogate pairs lasting at least
  $\tau$, and $\rho_{0,d}(\tau)=M_d(\tau)/S_d$ the chance intervals per second of exposure.
\end{enumerate}
Over the archive, with $N(\tau)$ the number of real intervals of the pairs of every
$\mathcal{P}_d$ lasting at least $\tau$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fdr}
  C(\tau)=\sum_d \rho_{0,d}(\tau)\,H_d,\qquad
  \widehat{\mathrm{FDR}}(\tau)=\frac{C(\tau)}{N(\tau)},\qquad
  \tau^*=\min\bigl\{\tau:\ \widehat{\mathrm{FDR}}(\tau)\le q\bigr\}.
\end{equation}
Dates without surrogates add to $N$ only, and $\tau$ runs over the grid $\{0,5,\dots,14\,400\}$~s.
The value $q=0.1$ is chosen in Section~\ref{sec:q}.

\begin{inwords}
$C(\tau)$ is the number of intervals lasting at least $\tau$ that chance alone would give the
real pairs. Dividing it by the number actually observed gives the share of chance intervals among
them; $\tau^*$ is the shortest duration at which that share falls to $q$, one in ten.
\end{inwords}

This is the estimate of the false discovery rate of SAM~\cite{tusher2001}, the number of
discoveries expected by chance over the number observed, with time shifts in place of the
permutations.

Most contacts are chance (Figure~\ref{fig:fdr}): 74\% of all pair intervals with
$\delta=2$~min (77\% with 5~min), 47\% of those lasting 10~min, 16\% of those lasting 30~min.
Short contacts therefore say little; only long ones are mostly real. This is why $\tau^*$ is
tens of minutes: it is the duration at which the real contacts outnumber chance by the factor
$1/q$. Resampling the dates 200 times moves $\tau^*$ by a few minutes (Table~\ref{tab:q}), so
the curve, and not the noise of the surrogates, sets it.

\subsection{How much the shift matters}\label{sec:shift-classes}

The false discovery rate was recomputed with only the surrogate pairs of each date whose shift
falls in one class, each class with its own exposure $S_d$ and intervals. The classes are
compared on the 2\,737 dates (of 6\,209) that have surrogates in each of them
(Table~\ref{tab:shift-classes}).

Short shifts give 2.8 times the chance of long ones at 10~min and 5.7 times at 30~min, and
$\tau^*$ goes from 23~min with shifts of an hour or more to 53~min with shifts under 30~min; with
all the shifts it is 38~min, as on the whole archive. Short shifts keep the weather of the hour,
but they also bring within $\delta$ the pilots who follow each other a few minutes apart, and the
delay profile shows that these are many: real correspondences are still 2 to 3 times chance
between 195 and 500~s (Section~\ref{sec:delta-choice}). The two effects cannot be separated here.
The length of the shift decides what counts as chance and moves $\tau^*$ by more than a factor
two; we keep the shifts that follow the takeoff delays of the day.
