\begin{abstract}
\noindent
To compare how paraglider pilots move in a group and alone, we must know, at every instant of
every flight, whether the pilot flies in a group and with whom. Three difficulties stand in the
way, and the method answers each with one idea. Pilots who fly together follow the same route
minutes apart: two pilots are in contact when one of them is where the other was, or will be, a
few minutes earlier or later, within a flat box of tolerances and with headings compared only in
glide, after the following with a local delay of Li, Wu and Crofoot~\cite{li2013}. Contact is not
transitive and changes in time: at each instant the configurations are the connected components of
the contact graph, organised in groups and episodes as in the grouping structure of Buchin et
al.~\cite{buchin2015}. Pilots also meet by chance on the same ridges and in the same thermals:
copies of the flights shifted in time measure chance on the data, and a contact counts only if it
lasts long enough for at most one in ten to be chance. On the FFVL archive the delay is measured at
2~min and a contact must last 38~min; 21.7\% of the paraglider flights and 6.9\% of the hang-glider
flights then fly in a group at some point, in 17\,169 episodes. The groups are mostly pairs; their
pilots keep the same pace with no extra pull between them, their sizes change one pilot at a time,
and pilots return to the same partners more than chance explains. The output is a few tables that
label every instant of every flight.
\end{abstract}
