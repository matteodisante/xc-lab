\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We describe a method that decides, for every flight and every instant, whether a paraglider
pilot flies in a group or alone and, in the first case, with which other pilots. Two pilots are
in contact when one of them is where the other was, or will be, a few minutes earlier or later.
This is the proximity with a local delay of Li, Wu and Crofoot~\cite{li2013}, adapted to thermal
flight in three ways: a vertical tolerance separates pilots who circle in the same thermal at
distant heights; headings are compared only in glide; the maximum delay is bounded by
the visual range and measured on the data. At each instant the groups are the connected components of the contact graph. As
in the grouping structure of Buchin et al.~\cite{buchin2015}, a configuration is a fixed set of
flights over a maximal interval, and an episode is a chain of configurations that share members.
Contacts are found in two stages, a coarse filter that provably loses none and an exact scan;
their minimum duration is calibrated on a null model that shifts tracks in time. On the FFVL
archive most short contacts are chance: the maximum delay is measured at 2~min, and a contact must last
38~min for at most one in ten to be chance. With these values 21.7\% of the paraglider flights
and 6.9\% of the hang-glider flights fly in a group at some point, in 17\,169 episodes. The
horizontal and vertical tolerances show no plateau on the data and are declared as definitions.
The groups are mostly pairs; their pilots keep the same pace rather than being pulled together,
their sizes change one pilot at a time, and pilots return to the same partners more than chance
explains.
The output is a
few tables that label every instant of every flight, so that group flight can be compared with
solo flight.
\end{abstract}
