\section{Tuning on the data}\label{sec:tuning}

The method was run on all the cleaned flights of the FFVL archive (162\,485 flights of
6\,209 dates), first with the starting values of Table~\ref{tab:notation} and then with the
values chosen below. The companion note \emph{Assessing the group identification}
(\texttt{method\_assessment.pdf}, same folder) gives every step and figure; this section keeps
what the method needs. The run read the cleaned tracks of the old repository (soaring, tag
\texttt{legacy-final}), so the phases needed by the heading condition exist on 1~Hz flights
only.

\subsection{Most contacts are chance}

The null model of Section~\ref{sec:null} was run on every date, with at most 200 surrogate
pairs per date. Since the surrogates are a sample of the pairs of each date, chance is
counted date by date: $\rho_{0,d}(\tau)H_d$ summed over the dates $d$, instead of one $\rho_0$
for the whole archive. Most contacts turn out to be chance (Figure~\ref{fig:tuning-fdr}):
with the starting values the false discovery rate is 0.77 over all pair intervals and still
0.32 for those lasting 30~min, so $q=0.05$ needs $\tau^*=89.5$~min. Pilots who never interact
meet all the same: they take off from the same site, follow the same valleys and climb in the
same house thermals. Short contacts therefore say little.

\subsection{The delay is measured}

The bound~\eqref{eq:delta} gives the largest delay at which two pilots can still see each
other; it does not say where following ends. To measure it, the correspondences $(t,t+l)$
of the real pairs and of their surrogates were counted at every delay $l$ up to 15~min, per
second of exposure, on 300 random dates (Figure~\ref{fig:tuning-delays}). Chance is flat in
$l$. The real pairs peak at a few seconds, with 5.6 times the correspondences of chance, and
their ratio to chance falls below 4 at 105~s, below 3 at 195~s and below 2 at 500~s. The
excess has a fast part, pilots flying together, and a slow tail that has not ended at
15~min, pilots who use the same thermals at about the same time without following each
other. The contacts that last longest agree: those of at least 90~min have a median delay of
25~s, 90\% of them within 2~min.

We take $\delta=2$~min, where real correspondences are still at least 3.7 times chance. Two
pilots in contact are then at most $\varepsilon_h+v_{\max}\delta\approx3.8$~km apart at the
same instant, within the visual range of~\eqref{eq:delta}. A shorter delay also lets fewer
chance matches in: at $q=0.05$, $\tau^*$ falls from 89.5 to 51.9~min.

\subsection{The false discovery rate}

$q$ trades the number of groups we see against how many of them are chance
(Table~\ref{tab:tuning-q}). We take $q=0.1$: at most one contact in ten is chance, and
$\tau^*=38.1$~min, between 37.0 and 41.1~min when the dates are resampled.

\subsection{No plateau}

Changing one tolerance at a time on 300 dates, with $\tau^*$ recalibrated each time, moves the
fraction of flight time in a group $f_G$ by up to a factor three, mostly through $\tau^*$;
$\theta_{\max}$ changes nothing, since contacts in glide are few. There is no plateau
(Section~\ref{sec:plateau}): $\varepsilon_h$, $\varepsilon_z$ and $g$ are definitions to declare.
They keep their starting values. Their profiles against chance, the analogues of
Figure~\ref{fig:tuning-delays} in the companion note, decay smoothly with no edge: at 500~m and
300~m real pairs are about 1.5 times chance, and the gaps of real and chance contacts have the
same lengths.

\begin{remark}[what the null model calls real]
The shift of a surrogate is at least $2\delta+\Delta$, but it follows the takeoff delays of the
day: over the whole archive its median is 58~min, and 90\% of the shifts lie between 8~min
and 4.4~h. Shifts of this size also remove the synchronisation of the pilots with the weather of
the hour, such as which thermals work and the cloud base. What survives $\tau^*$ therefore includes pilots who share
the same thermals at the same time without following each other: the shared air of
Remark~\ref{rem:notions}, which is the notion of contact we adopt, rather than a proof of
interaction.
\end{remark}
