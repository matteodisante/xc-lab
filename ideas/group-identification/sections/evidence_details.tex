\section{Further evidence on the parameters}\label{app:evidence}

Two measurements support Section~\ref{sec:tuning} without changing its choices.

\paragraph{The delay inside the contacts.} Figure~\ref{fig:ell} gives the whole distribution behind
Table~\ref{tab:ell}: short intervals spread over the window and pile up at its edge, long ones
gather at small delays.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{figures/assessment_ell.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of the $|\ell_{\rm med}|$ of the pair intervals, by their duration
($\delta=5$~min, whole archive).}
\label{fig:ell}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The distance at the same instant.} Is there a distance beyond which two pilots stop
being closer than chance? We counted the distance between the two pilots at every grid instant
when both fly after departure, without requiring a correspondence, for all the real pairs and for
the surrogates, on the dates of Figure~\ref{fig:delays} that have surrogates (210 of 300,
58\,901~h of real exposure). Real pairs are much closer than chance at short distance and have no
edge (Figure~\ref{fig:separation}): the ratio $g(d)$ is 10.4 within 100~m, below 2 from 500~m,
below 1.5 from 900~m, below 1.1 from 2.7~km and below 1 from 5.7~km. Real pairs spend 6.6\% of
their time together within 1~km of each other, against 2.8\% by chance. Beyond 6~km $g$ falls
slightly below 1, because both distributions sum to one.

The excess reaches several kilometres, beyond the visual range of 1.5~km of
Section~\ref{sec:delta}. Since the surrogates also change the hour, this excess may hold the
weather of the hour as well as sight, so it bounds neither the visual range nor the range of
following.

\end{figure}
