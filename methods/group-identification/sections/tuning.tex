\section{The parameters on the data}\label{sec:tuning}

Which values should the parameters take, and which of them can the data decide? For each one we
ask a question, compare real pairs with chance (Section~\ref{sec:null}) and choose; the profiles
use a sample of 300 random dates, the choices of $\delta$ and $q$ the whole archive.
Table~\ref{tab:parameters} sorts the parameters in three kinds. The \emph{tolerances} $\delta$,
$\varepsilon_h$, $\varepsilon_z$, $\theta_{\max}$ and $g$ say when two positions match. The
\emph{duration} $\tau^*$ says how long a match must last, and follows from the share $q$ of chance
contacts we accept (Section~\ref{sec:fdr}). The \emph{fixed settings}, in the lower part of the
table, make the computation exact or fast and are not tuned.\label{sec:fixed}

\subsection{The maximum delay \texorpdfstring{$\delta$}{delta}}\label{sec:delta-choice}

How far behind can a pilot be and still fly with the other? The visual range bounds $\delta$ at
about 5~min (Section~\ref{sec:delta}); here the data say how long the delay between two pilots who
fly together actually is.

\paragraph{The delay profile.} For every delay $l$ we counted the correspondences $(t,t+l)$ of the
real pairs and of the surrogate pairs, per second of exposure, with a window of 15~min on 300
random dates (58\,910~h of real exposure), and took their ratio:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:profile}
  R(l)=\frac{\text{real correspondences at delay } l \text{ per second of exposure}}
             {\text{chance correspondences at delay } l \text{ per second of exposure}} .
\end{equation}
Chance is flat in the delay, as expected: a shifted copy has no preferred delay. The real pairs
peak at a few seconds, where they have 5.6 times the correspondences of chance, and decay: $R$
falls below 4 at 105~s, below 3 at 195~s and below 2 at 500~s, and is still 1.5 at 15~min
(Figure~\ref{fig:delays}). The excess has two parts. The fast one, within 2--3~min, is pilots
flying together. The slow one, which has not ended at 15~min, is pilots who use the same thermals
at about the same time without following each other, a synchrony that the shifts of the
surrogates remove. A rule such as ``the delay that holds 95\% of the excess'' would pick 13~min,
because it counts the slow tail; so we read the ratio instead.

\paragraph{The delay inside the contacts.} We computed $|\ell_{\rm med}|$ (Appendix~\ref{sec:lag})
for every pair interval of the run with $\delta=5$~min, whose window is wide enough to show where
the delays fall: 965\,770 intervals, of which 169\,686 have all their instants in search and no
delay. The longer the contact, the shorter the delay (Table~\ref{tab:ell}; the whole distribution
is in Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}). A pair that only shares route and weather matches at any delay
of the window, and its closest correspondence is often at the largest ones: short intervals pile
up at the edge. The contacts of at least 90~min, all above $\tau^*$ of this run (89.5~min), have a
median delay of 25~s, and 90\% of them lie within 2~min. Long contacts are pilots a few tens of
seconds apart on the same path.

\paragraph{Choice.} We take $\delta=2$~min: within it the real correspondences are at least 3.7
times chance, and it contains nine in ten of the long contacts. A shorter delay also shortens
$\tau^*$, since fewer chance matches enter: from 89.5 to 51.9~min at $q=0.05$ (Table~\ref{tab:q}).

The delay also bounds the distance between two pilots in contact at the same instant: at most
$\varepsilon_h+v_{\max}\delta\approx0.5+27.8\times120/1000\approx3.8$~km, below the 6~km we would
still call a group; a group, a chain of pairs, can be wider. Even with the 15~min window, 58\% of
the instants in contact have the two pilots within 2~km and 94\% within 6~km
(Figure~\ref{fig:delays}, right).

\subsection{The tolerances against chance}\label{sec:tolerances}

Is there a distance, a height difference or a gap beyond which real pairs look like chance? Such
an edge would give the natural value of $\varepsilon_h$, $\varepsilon_z$ or $g$. In analogy with
the delay profile, with $\delta=2$~min and the null model of the chosen run, on the 210 of the 300
dates that have surrogates, we measured at every instant $t$ of pilot $i$ the smallest horizontal
distance between $i$ at $t$ and $j$ at $t+l$, $|l|\le\delta$, over the delays that pass the
vertical and heading conditions; the smallest height difference, over the delays that pass the
horizontal and heading conditions; and the gaps between consecutive instants of contact of a
pair.

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/assessment_tolerances.pdf}
\caption{The tolerance profiles on 210 dates. Left and middle: real pairs over chance, per
second of exposure, by the smallest horizontal distance and the smallest height difference to
the path of the other pilot within $\delta$; dotted, the values in use. Right: the lengths of
the gaps between instants of contact, as a share of all gaps (the 8\% of real and 9\% of chance
gaps longer than 30~min are not drawn); dotted, $g$.}
\label{fig:tolerances}
\end{figure}

None of the three has an edge (Figure~\ref{fig:tolerances}). The horizontal ratio is 6.3 within
25~m, below 2 from 250~m, below 1.5 from 500~m, and still 1.13 between 2.5 and 3~km. The vertical
ratio is 4.0 within 25~m and below 2 from 150~m; from 275~m on it stays near 1.5 (1.51 on average
up to 1~km), the excess that the horizontal condition alone gives. Both values in use sit where
real pairs are about 1.5 times chance.

Real pairs have 1.46 times the gaps of chance per hour, because they have more contact, but the
lengths of the gaps are the same: the shares of each class differ by at most 2 points. The gaps
come from how pilots fly in and out of each other's path, the same in real and in chance contacts.
The distance between the two pilots at the same instant, counted without requiring a
correspondence, has no edge either (Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}).

\paragraph{Choice.} The data single out none of $\varepsilon_h$, $\varepsilon_z$ and $g$: we keep
500~m, 300~m and 120~s and declare them as definitions.

\subsection{One parameter at a time}\label{sec:plateau}

Does the result depend on the exact thresholds? A plateau is a range of values over which the
result does not change; a value inside it makes the result insensitive to the exact threshold. We
measure the result by $f_G$, the fraction of the flight time after departure spent in a group:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fg}
  f_G=\frac{\sum_i\bigl|\{t\in T^\dep_i:\ \gamma_i(t)\notin\{\st{L},\st{S}\}\}\bigr|}{\sum_i|T^\dep_i|}.
\end{equation}
We changed one parameter at a time on 300 random dates, recalibrating $\tau^*$ each time, with
$\delta=5$~min (Table~\ref{tab:plateau} in Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}). $\theta_{\max}$ changes nothing: contacts in glide are
few. The others move $f_G$ by up to a factor three, mostly through $\tau^*$. There is no plateau,
in agreement with the profiles: the thresholds decide the result, and the thesis must say so.

The check is coarse. On 300 dates $\tau^*$ is noisy (126~min with the starting values against 89.5
on the whole archive, and six settings stop at the same surrogate interval); a conclusive check
needs the whole archive for each setting, about one hour each. It was not repeated with
$\delta=2$~min.

\subsection{The false discovery rate \texorpdfstring{$q$}{q}}\label{sec:q}

How much chance do we accept among the contacts we keep? A smaller $q$ keeps only very long
contacts and few groups, almost all of them real; a larger $q$ sees more groups, with more chance
among them. Table~\ref{tab:q} and Figure~\ref{fig:q} give the trade-off.

\paragraph{Choice.} We take $q=0.1$: at most one contact in ten is chance, and $\tau^*=38.1$~min.
Going to 0.2 would lower $\tau^*$ to 26~min but would let chance make one contact in five.

\paragraph{What we keep.} $\delta=2$~min and $q=0.1$ are our choices on the data, and give
$\tau^*=38.1$~min; $\varepsilon_h$, $\varepsilon_z$ and $g$ are definitions, and $\theta_{\max}$
does not matter.
